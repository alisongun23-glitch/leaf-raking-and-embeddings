# Rake from the leaves, plan the piles

No published work studies this raking game, but its rules split cleanly into pieces that eleven neighbouring literatures have already solved, because **every point, pile and raker position always sits on one of the original N sites, so the raker's reach graph is one fixed k-disk graph that only ever loses vertices.** That fact settles safety: collecting points leaf-first along a spanning tree, and founding piles on bridge points (the user's "frontier point r" move), provably never strands the raker when l = 0, and a linear-time exact test vets any capsule stroke otherwise. It also yields a sharp feasibility rule: **when l ≤ k/2, no capsule can reach across a gap in the reach graph, so k must be at least the longest edge M of the Euclidean minimum spanning tree** — about 1.6–1.8 mean point spacings for uniform fields and 1.2–5.6 times that under clustering — and each pile's members must form a connected group, which makes k ≥ M necessary but not sufficient. Exact optimization is out of reach (even sweeping points into one target with an unbounded broom has open complexity), so benchmarks should score algorithms against certified lower bounds: the exact identity strokes = N − n − captures, an area bound on travel with service radius k + l, and transport and Lagrangian bounds on work. Realistic "mildly grouped" fields come from a Thomas cluster process conditioned on exactly N points, tuned with one pair-correlation dial and accepted when the edge-corrected Clark–Evans ratio lands between 0.85 and 0.95. The user's "at least t" rule changes the notes' exact-t conclusions in one decisive way: the brittle "never overfill a pile" constraint becomes an **overflow budget of s = N − n·t points**, which removes the overfill deadlock, legalizes snowballing and full-pile merges, turns pile siting into Bradley et al.'s minimum-size clustering, and adds a single new failure mode — starving the remaining piles of mass — while N = n·t collapses everything back to the exact case.

## The game reduces to deleting vertices from one fixed disk graph

Under the user's rules nothing ever occupies a new location. Piles are founded on points, a stroke delivers the selected point or pile into the pile the raker stands on, a merge delivers one pile into another, and the raker only steps onto objects, so **every object and every raker position is always one of the original N sites, and the occupied set only shrinks**†. (A dagger marks derivations made by the research team or for this report rather than published results; they deserve independent review.) The raker's options are therefore always an induced subgraph of one static graph G_k(P), which joins two original points whenever they lie within k of each other: a stroke deletes the selected object's site plus every captured loose point, founding a pile only relabels a site, and walking deletes nothing. Four consequences shape everything that follows. The simulator can build G_k once, using a uniform grid of cell size k so each neighbourhood lives in a 3×3 block of cells, and answer every later safety question with a masked graph search. Reachability, once lost, never returns. Because the selected object lies within k and the destination is the raker's own pile, **every stroke is at most k long and its capsule lies inside the disk of radius k + l around the raker**. And because capsules sweep only loose points, piles are permanent stepping stones that disappear only when deliberately dragged or merged.

The same bookkeeping gives an exact count†. Each stroke or merge removes the selected object plus its captured loose points, founding removes nothing, and play runs from N objects down to n piles, so **strokes (merges included) = N − n − C, where C is the total number of captured points**, regardless of how many piles were founded along the way. Two corollaries bear on the weighted objective: with l = 0 every valid plan uses exactly N − n strokes, so the stroke weight matters only once capsules have width; and extra stepping-stone piles cost no strokes, only work and travel. The literal stranding rule — never be left with nothing reachable while work remains — is too weak to enforce, since a raker can keep neighbours while a loose point elsewhere has been cut off for good. The invariant worth enforcing is that every loose point, and every pile still needing points or a merge, lies in the raker's connected component; the section on k and l shows when that test is exact.

Read together, these results set expectations about optimality. No hardness proof exists for this game, and its closest geometric ancestor is unresolved: whether shortest line-sweep solutions always use polynomially many sweeps is listed as open problem CG-Open26 ([∃R compendium](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18006)). The PSPACE-completeness of Sokoban and Push-1 rests on reversible box motion among fixed walls, which this game lacks. Here every stroke permanently removes an object, so a successful play has at most about 2N state-changing actions (at most N − n strokes and N foundings), a plan is a short certificate whose within-k and within-l tests are exact on rational coordinates, and **deciding feasibility lies in NP**†. Optimizing is harder in practice: the siting subproblem alone is a size-constrained median problem, and for the upper-capacity version studied most, known constant-factor algorithms must bend either the capacities or the number of centres ([Demirci & Li](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ICALP.2016.73)), while pushing material into capacity-limited piles is NP-complete ([Arkin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0603026)). The practical conclusion matches the one Sokoban and SameGame research reached: exact search on tiny instances, heuristics plus lower-bound gaps at scale.

## Thomas clusters conditioned on N give a one-dial generator

Every candidate model follows one recipe — draw a random intensity, then scatter points from it — and each fits in a few dozen lines of JavaScript. The Thomas process places Poisson parents at intensity κ and gives each a Poisson(μ) number of offspring displaced by Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ ([spatstat rThomas](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/spatstat.random/html/rThomas.html)); the Matérn process scatters offspring uniformly in a disc of radius R ([rMatClust](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/spatstat.random/html/rMatClust.html)); the log-Gaussian Cox process (LGCP) exponentiates a Gaussian random field, producing smooth hot spots instead of discrete clumps ([rLGCP](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/spatstat.random/html/rLGCP.html)); and a Gaussian mixture over uniform "clutter" offers a direct number-of-clumps dial ([Dasgupta & Raftery](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/dasgupta1998.pdf)). The natural design dial is the pair-correlation excess at zero distance, g(0) − 1, which has closed forms: **1/(4πκσ²) for Thomas** ([Waagepetersen](https://people.math.aau.dk/~rw/Talks/rennes.pdf)), e^{σ²} − 1 for an LGCP with log-variance σ² ([Palm-distribution tutorial](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.05871)), and 1/(πκR²) for Matérn†, which matches Thomas when R = 2σ. A uniform background holding a fraction w₀ of the points multiplies the excess by (1 − w₀)²†. Three further dials complete the generator: κA sets the number of clumps, μ = (1 − w₀)N/(κA) the points per clump, and w₀ the stepping stones between clumps.

The literature never defines "mild" numerically, so the research team calibrated it with seeded Monte Carlo runs (mulberry32 generator, unit square, exactly N points; scripts and output are saved in research_notes/Raking points into piles/sims/). A dial of g(0) − 1 ≈ 0.5 gave edge-corrected Clark–Evans ratios of R ≈ 0.91–0.93, values of 1–2 gave 0.69–0.90, and values above about 6 gave 0.33–0.77 ([team simulation](../research_notes/Raking%20points%20into%20piles/sims/sim_output.txt)). The proposed band for "slightly grouped" is **R ≈ 0.85–0.95**, reached by Thomas fields with s = σ√κ ≳ 0.3, or by a tighter s ≈ 0.2 diluted with 30–50% background. The dial does not predict R across models — an LGCP with g(0) − 1 = 6.4 still scored 0.77 while a Thomas field at 2.0 scored 0.70, because the LGCP spreads its correlation over a larger scale — so the generator should gate every instance on its measured R instead of trusting the dial. That measurement needs the Donnelly edge correction for rectangles, since the raw ratio is biased upward when true neighbours fall outside the window ([spatstat clarkevans](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/spatstat.explore/html/clarkevans.html)); uniform points scored 1.034 uncorrected and 1.003 corrected at N = 200. The table also previews the next section by listing each preset's median minimum reach M.

Exact counts are cheap†. Given its parents, a Thomas field with Poisson offspring is a Poisson process with intensity Λ(x), so proposing parents from the prior and accepting them with probability (Λ(W)/N)^N·e^{N − Λ(W)} conditions exactly on N points, after which the N points are drawn independently from Λ. The acceptance rate is about 1/√(1 + μ(1 − w₀)) — predicted 0.333 and observed 0.35–0.37 at N = 200 and μ = 8 — which makes the method **roughly 35 times cheaper than rerunning until the count happens to equal N** ([team simulation](../research_notes/Raking%20points%20into%20piles/sims/sim_output.txt)). Parents belong in a window expanded by 4σ for Thomas (R for Matérn), and offspring that land outside the region must be rejected, never clipped, because clipping stacks points along the border. Under the user's rule exact N is no longer forced, since any N ≥ n·t is legal; fixing N still keeps paired comparisons clean, and the slack s = N − n·t joins the clustering dial as a second instance parameter.

No source in the notes measured clustering parameters for real leaf fall, so realism beyond "mild" is a modelling judgment: Thomas or Matérn clumps with a canopy-sized radius for leaves under trees, an LGCP for wind-driven drifts, and extra intensity along fences, which robot experiments show behave like giant clusters that trap material ([Song, Kim & Shell](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)). Random fields should be paired with adversarial instances that exercise the safety logic: chains, single-point bridges, dumbbells, rings and long-armed stars. Every random stream must come from an explicit seeded generator, because JavaScript's `Math.random` cannot be seeded ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Math/random)), with separate streams for instances and for algorithm randomness so that changing an algorithm never changes the instances.

## Reach must clear the spanning-tree bottleneck, which clustering inflates up to 5.6-fold

The smallest k that connects G_k(P) is exactly M, the longest edge of the Euclidean minimum spanning tree (EMST): Kruskal's algorithm, run over all edges no longer than k, ends with exactly the components of G_k†. M costs O(N log N) to compute because the EMST lies inside the Delaunay triangulation ([Wikipedia: EMST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_minimum_spanning_tree)), and the Delaunator library triangulated 100,000 points in 82 ms on 2017 hardware ([Delaunator](https://github.com/mapbox/delaunator)). Whether k ≥ M is actually required depends on l, and a short argument makes the dependence sharp†. A point p outside the raker's component lies more than k from every object in it; every stroke joins two such objects at most k apart, so the nearest point of the stroke segment lies within k/2 of an endpoint, and p sits more than k/2 from the segment. **When l ≤ k/2, therefore, no capsule ever captures a point across a reach gap: G_k(P) must be connected, and any loose point that becomes disconnected during play is a permanent deadlock.** The same geometry shows that a captured point always lies within l + k/2 ≤ k of one end of its stroke, so below this width the static graph governs everything. Above it, the initial condition weakens to "every point outside the start's component lies within l of a segment joining two of its points at most k apart," which a grid search can test.

At game sizes M runs well above textbook formulas. For uniform points in a square, the team's runs put the **median minimum reach at 1.54–1.79 mean spacings √(A/N) for N = 50–2,000, with the 95th percentile 1.2–1.3 times the median**, equivalent to 7.5–10 neighbours on average at threshold ([team calibration](../research_notes/Raking%20points%20into%20piles/sims/calib.mjs)). The asymptotic law — nπM² − log n converges to a Gumbel distribution ([Penrose](https://people.bath.ac.uk/masmdp/ab/longest.html)), consistent with Gupta and Kumar's threshold πr²N = ln N + c ([Goel, Rai & Krishnamachari](https://web.stanford.edu/~ashishg/papers/gnrn.pdf)) — falls 13–28% short at N = 100–2,000 for two reasons. Border points have only half or a quarter of a neighbourhood, an effect that is "not only relevant but dominant" for full connectivity ([Coon, Dettmann & Georgiou](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.3123)); and isolated pairs and small groups disconnect the graph too, so at N = 200 and a reach where 49% of instances had no isolated point, only 21% were connected ([team diagnostic](../research_notes/Raking%20points%20into%20piles/sims/diag.mjs)). Percolation is no substitute, since a giant component already appears at a mean degree of about 4.51, four times the disk-percolation constant 1.128 ([Mertens & Moore](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.4936)). Clustering multiplies M (see the table) and changes its cause: in moderate and strong Thomas fields a lone point sets the bottleneck only 5–15% of the time, the rest being gaps between groups, so under strong clustering M stalls near the parent spacing 1/√κ as N grows (0.350 to 0.311 between N = 200 and 1,000). A 30% uniform background supplies stepping stones and cuts the moderate-clustering penalty from 2.57× to 1.48×. No theorem covers full connectivity over Thomas or Matérn points — clustering does not even always hurt percolation ([Błaszczyszyn & Yogeshwaran](https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.2227)) — so the simulator should compute M for every instance and never infer it from a formula.

k ≥ M is necessary but not sufficient, because the size rules impose a partition†. With l ≤ k/2, each point reaches its final pile through drags no longer than k or captures within k of a stroke end, and every relay pile it passes through ends inside the same final pile, since piles never split; so **the members of each final pile must form a connected subgraph of G_k**. Three points spaced k from a hub at 120° intervals, with n = 2 and t = 2, have k = M yet no valid plan: the hub can join only one pile, and the remaining two points sit √3·k apart. The argument yields a cheap screen from the block-cut tree that one Hopcroft–Tarjan pass computes in linear time ([Hopcroft & Tarjan](https://cacm.acm.org/research/algorithm-447-efficient-algorithms-for-graph-manipulation)): every dangling piece with fewer than t points behind a cut vertex must join that vertex's pile, and if this forced pile leaves fewer than (n − 1)·t points elsewhere, the instance is infeasible. Beyond such screens the only general certificate is constructive — a safe planner that finishes proves feasibility — so instances should be labelled feasible, infeasible or undetermined rather than assumed solvable.

Play itself erodes connectivity, and the shape of the erosion matters. Removing 30% of 1,000 uniform points at random raised the bottleneck 1.18×, a compact interior hole 1.03×, many short rake-shaped capsules 1.33×, and one full-width band 0.3 wide 5.5× ([team simulation](../research_notes/Raking%20points%20into%20piles/sims/sim_output.txt)); network studies likewise find geographically localized damage qualitatively different from random failure ([Berezin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.0996)). The algorithmic lesson is that parallel strokes spanning the field cut it in two unless a pile is left in each swath as a bridge. A useful live gauge is the "frog distance" — the longest edge needed to connect all remaining work, from Kruskal over the presorted static edges restricted to alive sites — whose margin below k shows how close the raker is to stranding; alongside it, the interface can draw the bottleneck edge that makes an instance impossible, colour components, and mark articulation points among loose points as "rake later."

The main difficulty knob for k is therefore the slack k/M, and three regime boundaries help set it. Near k ≈ M piles are too sparse to bridge anything: piles about √(t/ρ) apart (ρ = N/A) form a connected backbone only when t ≲ d/π, where d = ρπk² is the mean neighbour count — roughly t ≲ 3 at threshold-level d ≈ 10† — so loose points must serve as temporary bridges and removal order becomes critical. At **k ≥ 3M**, G_k contains the cube of the EMST, and the cube of any connected graph is Hamiltonian-connected ([Karaganis and Sekanina, as reproved in arXiv 2206.02385](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.02385)), so a single-pass never-stranding order exists between any two points even without stepping-stone piles†. In between, the ratio of ρπk² to t predicts behaviour: near 1, local seeding naturally builds piles of about t; well below 1, piles need multi-hop relays; well above 1, one stroke can capture more than a pile's worth.

For l, **k/2 is the natural ceiling**. Below it the connectivity test is exact, and a full-length capsule already covers a fraction (2/π)(l/k) + (l/k)² of the raker's reach disk — 22% at l = k/4 and 57% at l = k/2 — so a full stroke captures about ρ_loose·(2lk + πl²) points on average†. Relative to the mean spacing δ = √(A/N), l ≪ δ turns the game into pure transport with strokes ≈ N − n, while l between δ/2 and δ yields several captures per long stroke. The notes, assuming exactly t per pile, treated wide capsules as dangerous because one capture into a full pile overfills it permanently; under the user's rule that danger becomes a budget (final section), so wide capsules are limited mainly by bridge deletion and by how quickly they spend the overflow budget. As judgment rather than measurement, sensible presets are a comfortable k ≈ 1.3–1.5 M with l ≈ k/4, a tight k ≈ 1.0–1.1 M with l ≤ k/4, and a sweep-heavy l ≈ k/2.

## Leaf-first peeling keeps the raker safe, and global siting decides cost

### Remove leaves, keep bridges

Graph theory supplies the safety guarantee almost directly. Every connected graph with at least two vertices has at least two non-cut vertices — the leaves of any spanning tree ([Rutgers notes](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~jsb274/graphtheory/hw/hw6.pdf)) — and an elimination order keeps every intermediate remainder connected exactly when each removed vertex still has a neighbour that is removed later or kept†, so reverse breadth-first order, reverse depth-first order and spanning-tree post-order are all safe. The constructive strategy is **"rake from the parent"**: peel the leaves of a spanning tree of G_k rooted at the intended last pile, standing on each leaf's parent, which is alive and within k because tree edges are. The parent must be a pile before the raker can rake into it, which is exactly the user's "turn a point into a new pile" move. The user's frontier case — only one point r left within k of the current pile — is the moment the current pile has itself become a leaf, so the safe move is to found r, or drag the pile onto r, rather than consume the bridge. With l = 0 this order never strands the raker, and an Euler tour of the tree bounds travel by twice the tree's length. Capsules disturb tree-following strokes only near their ends: because the EMST lies inside the relative neighbourhood graph ([Wikipedia: EMST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_minimum_spanning_tree)), the lune between an edge's endpoints is empty, so **a drag along an EMST edge can capture only points within 2l of its two endpoints**†. When catchments sit farther apart than k, the research team recommends keeping "breadcrumb" loose points between them and collecting those last while retreating toward the final pile.

For arbitrary strokes an exact check is cheap. Deleting a set D from a connected graph leaves it connected exactly when all surviving neighbours of D, plus the raker, lie in one component, because every remaining component must touch D; with persistent piles the test relaxes to "every component holding work contains the raker"†. Running one breadth-first search from each boundary vertex in round-robin, merging fronts as they touch, answers "safe" after a local exploration and "unsafe" after exploring the smaller severed piece, and a first pass restricted to alive sites within l + 2k of the capsule gives a sufficient fast path. Articulation points from one Hopcroft–Tarjan pass reject single-vertex removals in constant time ([Hopcroft & Tarjan](https://cacm.acm.org/research/algorithm-447-efficient-algorithms-for-graph-manipulation)), and a complete recorded plan can be verified backwards with union–find in near-linear time. At N = 5,000 the k-disk graph has roughly 20,000–100,000 edges, so one masked search should take about a millisecond or less in JavaScript — an estimate, not a measurement — and polylogarithmic dynamic-connectivity structures for disk graphs ([Kaplan et al.](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2022.49)) are unnecessary at this scale. One gap remains: the filter guarantees the raker never strands, but for l > 0 neither the notes nor this report could show that a safe, productive stroke always exists, so planners need lookahead or repair to escape dead ends.

Simpler rules fail for identifiable reasons. Nearest-first greedy removes the vertices beside the raker, which are often cut vertices, and nearest-neighbour tours run Θ(log n) times optimal even on Euclidean instances ([Hougardy & Wilde](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.2071)). If nothing persisted, never-stranding coverage would be a Hamiltonian path in a unit-disk graph, a problem already NP-complete on grid graphs ([MIT 6.890 notes](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.890/fall14/scribe/lec8.pdf)); persistent piles, which permit backtracking, are what make this game tractable. Constrained-exploration theory points the same way: tethered and fuel-limited robots explore a graph in Θ(|E|) edge traversals, only a constant factor above unconstrained exploration ([Duncan, Kobourov & Kumar](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/optimal-constrained-graph-exploration/)), which suggests safety should cost moderate rather than catastrophic extra travel when k comfortably exceeds M.

### Site piles first, rake along spokes, merge only for captures

Cost minimization splits into three levels — siting and assignment, a stroke plan per pile, and the route between piles — the same decomposition a real leaf-collection study used for sweepers, bag deposit points and trucks ([Delle Donne et al.](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/handle/2250/176108?show=full)). Siting must be global because local rules get the pile count wrong: in ant cemeteries the number of piles follows corpse density and arena size rather than any target ([Theraulaz et al.](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)), Lumer–Faieta ant clustering typically ends with too many clusters until ants remember where earlier drops went ([Ramos & Merelo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075)), and robot swarms that commit to shared cache sites converge significantly faster than purely probabilistic ones ([Vardy et al.](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/Vardy_2014_SwarmIntl.html)). Under the user's rule, siting is exactly Bradley et al.'s constrained k-means — n clusters, each receiving at least τ = t points — whose fixed-centre assignment is a min-cost flow with integral optima ([Bradley, Bennett & Demiriz](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-2000-65.pdf)). Raking work is linear in distance, so the arcs should carry Euclidean rather than squared distances and the centre update should be a discrete 1-median rather than a centroid; with linear costs the optimal cells are additively weighted and star-shaped about their own sites, so a straight drag never leaves its cell†. Sorting points along a Hilbert curve and cutting n consecutive blocks of about N/n points (each at least t, since N ≥ n·t) gives an O(N log N) seed and baseline, and Prins' "Split" dynamic program ([Prins](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0305-0548(03)00158-8)) can then optimize the cut points.

Within each catchment the transport arithmetic is unforgiving. Raking every point straight to its final site minimizes work, and **a relay pile never reduces work**: routing points x through relay s to final site f adds Σ(|x − s| + |s − f| − |x − f|) ≥ 0, which vanishes only when s lies on the straight spokes†. Merges therefore pay only through captures, shorter travel, or reach, since a catchment wider than k forces relays. Captures into a final pile save a stroke at no extra work, provided work charges each point its straight-line distance to the pile, so the ideal plan rakes along spokes, farthest point first, letting each capsule sweep the nearer points on its spoke; covering a disk-shaped catchment of radius R this way takes about πR/l spokes†. Swarm and field evidence supplies the endgame rules. Deposit on the largest pile still below t and never take from piles at or above it, which reproduces Kazadi's condition — removals per deposit falling with cluster size — that guarantees puck-clustering robots converge ([Song, Kim & Shell](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)). Merge unequal partial piles early, because two equal clusters on opposite sides of an arena stalled one robot run for 25.3 hours ([Holland & Melhuish](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)) and were the most common outcome in ant arenas ([Theraulaz et al.](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)). Work outside-in behind a monotone front, as e-puck robots did by circling the object cloud and nudging objects inward ([Gauci et al.](https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/22948/download)) and as municipal crews do by finishing a ward before moving on ([Latrobe](https://cityoflatrobe.com/services/public_works/leaf_collection.php)).

The research team's candidate heuristics, synthesized from that evidence and still untested in the simulator, trade these effects differently:

| Heuristic | Core move | Wins when | Loses when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planned sites and spokes | Site n piles by min-size flow; rake farthest-first along spokes; relay on spokes | Work-heavy weights; clumps holding roughly multiples of t | Clump sizes far from multiples of t force long boundary strokes |
| Windrow, then pile | Rake into rows of micro-piles spaced ≤ k, then merge along each row | Wide capsules and elongated fields; rows double as stepping-stone highways | Strong clustering; every point is handled twice |
| Grow the largest, capped | Drag the nearest safe point into the largest pile in reach still below t | ρπk² ≈ t; cheap and local | Leaves far-apart partial piles and the wrong pile count |
| Snowball | Drag the active pile along a tour so its capsule sweeps as it goes | Stroke-heavy weights, light work weight, l large relative to spacing | Mass-weighted work; overshoot under exact t |
| Perimeter inward | Peel the hull, dragging outermost points inward | Single convex blobs; interior bridges survive | Multi-cluster fields with gaps near k |
| Micro-piles, then merge | Many small piles first, then capacitated merging | Fragile connectivity | Double handling inflates work |

The weights decide which row wins, and nothing in the literature calibrates them: the notes found no peer-reviewed time-and-motion study of manual raking. Stroke count and distance genuinely conflict — in line sweeping, length-optimal solutions can need 3n/2 − O(1) sweeps while a four-sweep solution is at most 4/π longer ([Dumitrescu & Jiang](https://adriandumitrescu.org/sweep2.pdf)) — so the benchmark should sweep the weights or report Pareto fronts rather than a single scalar, remembering that with l = 0 the stroke term is a constant.

Search evidence from irreversible single-player puzzles is consistent. In SameGame, IDA* scored 22,354 points on the standard 20-position set, while single-player Monte Carlo tree search reached 78,012 and a memory-heavy beam search 84,414 ([Schadd et al.](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/KNOSYS_SameGame.pdf)). In Sokoban, IDA* with deadlock tables and macros solved 59 of the 90 standard levels, and feature-guided FESS solved all 90 in under four minutes ([Shoham & Elidan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.01904); [FESS](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~jonathan/publications/ai_publications/fess.pdf)). Deep reinforcement learning needed about 10⁹ steps to reach 99% on small 10×10 four-box levels ([Guez et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.03559)) and fell from 87% to 53% as boxes grew from four to seven ([Weber, Racanière et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.06203)). The raking game's horizon of roughly 250–2,500 strokes at N = 5,000, with 10²–10³ candidates per step (the team's estimate), is 4–40 times SameGame's 62 moves, so full-game tree search belongs offline. The recommended ladder starts with a safe greedy using exact filters. Next comes plan-then-repair: siting, per-site peel orders, whole-plan verification by reverse union–find, and local repair at the first violation, much like the Sokoban solver that fixes a box-packing order before searching. Then beam search over strokes, scored by the lower bounds below, and finally receding-horizon tree search or Nested Rollout Policy Adaptation deciding one to five strokes at a time, a method with a record on routing problems with time windows ([Cazenave & Teytaud](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01406457)). Exhaustive search over alive-set bitmasks on 12–16-point instances measures true optimality gaps; learned policies fit best as rollout priors.

## Certified bounds exist for every cost term, so score gaps rather than raw costs

No exact solver will reach N = 5,000, so the benchmark's denominators must be lower bounds; cost ÷ bound then caps each algorithm's optimality gap. Every term of the user's objective has one, though their strength varies widely:

| Cost term | Lower bound | Valid when | Cost at N = 5,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strokes | Exactly N − n − C; at least ⌈(N − n)/c⌉, with c = (most points in any disk of radius k + l centred on a site) − 1; at least \|Q\| − n for any set Q with pairwise gaps above k + 2l† | Always | Grid hashing, O(N × neighbours) |
| Travel | At least (U − 4πr²)/(4r), with r = k + l and U the area covered by radius-r disks around all points†; at least (πr/4)(m − 4) for m points pairwise more than 2r apart | Every point must come within k + l of the raker | Grid area estimate; greedy packing |
| Work, any pile sizes | Σ_j nn_j minus the n largest nn_j, where nn_j is point j's nearest-neighbour distance† | Each point is charged at least its displacement | O(N log N) |
| Work, at least t | Lagrangian bound with the minimum-size subproblem (final section)† | Same | O(N²) per iteration |
| Work, own final sites | Transport cost to the algorithm's own sites with its actual pile sizes; any dual-feasible weights | Same | O(N·n) per pass |

Strokes are the weakest-bounded term because one capsule can sweep many points, so the geometric bounds sit far below realistic counts, and an LP covering bound would be tighter but heavy for a browser. The identity makes this unimportant: **report sweep efficiency C/(N − n), which converts exactly into strokes.**

Travel needs care, because the bound the notes proposed fails in exactly the instances that matter. That bound subtracts 2(k + l) from every EMST edge and sums what remains; when k ≥ M every EMST edge is at most k, so **the MST-based travel bound is identically zero on every feasible instance with l ≤ k/2**†. The area bound replaces it. Every point comes within r = k + l of some raker position, because capsules lie inside the disk of radius k + l around the raker, so the radius-2r neighbourhood of the raker's path contains the radius-r disk around every point; Dumitrescu and Tóth's lemma bounds that neighbourhood's area by 2L(2r) + π(2r)² ([Dumitrescu & Tóth](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.6659)), giving L ≥ (U − 4πr²)/(4r)†. For 1,000 uniform points in a unit square with k = 1.2M and l = k/4, this evaluates to about 3.8 length units against a full travelling-salesman tour of about 0.7124·√(NA) ≈ 22.5 ([MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/TravelingSalesmanConstants.html)) — weak, but no longer zero. The packing bound of Dumitrescu and Mitchell gives similar values ([Dumitrescu & Mitchell](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.01640)). Offline, close-enough TSP solved exactly on growing witness subsets yields an increasing sequence of tighter bounds, the method that certified near-optimal lawn-mowing tours ([Fekete et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05891)).

Work bounds form a chain whose validity hinges on one definition the simulator must fix. The user's "distance each point is dragged" should charge each swept point its straight-line distance to the destination pile, which matches the phrase and keeps every transport bound valid by the triangle inequality; charging only from a point's projection onto the stroke would require subtracting up to l per swept point. With that definition, actual work ≥ transport cost to the algorithm's own final sites ≥ the best siting's cost ≥ the Lagrangian bound ≥ the nearest-neighbour bound†. The nearest-neighbour bound holds for any pile sizes but weakens as piles grow, to roughly 1.33/√t of typical cost†, so the Lagrangian bound — O(N²) per subgradient step, with an estimated 100–300 steps fitting in a Web Worker — should be the headline denominator. Writing actual work as (actual ÷ own-site transport) × (own-site transport ÷ Lagrangian) × Lagrangian separates relay and routing inefficiency from site-choice inefficiency†, which tells a developer which half of an algorithm to fix. Asymptotic baselines give context but no certificates: ideal equal-mass cells cost about 0.377·N·√(A/n), with unit-area constants of 0.3761 for a disk, 0.3772 for a hexagon and 0.3826 for a square by the team's numerical integration.

Metrics should make failures as informative as successes. Each run should record success or one failure mode — stranded, unreachable work, starvation (fewer than n piles can still reach t), wrong pile count, merge-range deadlock (surplus piles out of each other's reach) or timeout — plus strokes split into point strokes, pile drags, merges and foundings; travel; total drag length; work; sweep efficiency; the share of travel spent walking without raking; final pile sizes; wall-clock time per decision; and machine-independent counters such as safety checks and rollouts. Comparisons should be paired on identical seeded instances and analysed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for two algorithms, or the Friedman test with Holm or Nemenyi post-hoc tests for several ([Demšar](https://jmlr.org/papers/v7/demsar06a.html)); summarized with stratified-bootstrap intervals and interquartile means rather than point estimates ([Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13264)); and displayed as performance profiles ([Dolan & Moré](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0102001)), read with Gould and Scott's warning that profiles across several solvers mislead unless the best solver is dropped in sequence ([Gould & Scott](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/74694)). Success rates need McNemar's test on discordant pairs, and zero failures in n runs still leaves a 95% upper bound near 3/n. Because instances are cheap, a factorial or Latin-hypercube design over N ∈ {250, 1,000, 5,000}, k/M, l/k, t relative to mean degree, slack s/N and clustering band is affordable, with every result stratified by k/M, where failures are expected to concentrate; anytime planners belong on curves at fixed per-decision budgets such as 10 ms, 100 ms and 1 s. The simulator should check invariants after every action — pile sizes plus loose points sum to N, every selection lies within k, capsule contents match a brute-force reference — and save replay logs.

## "At least t" turns a brittle capacity into an overflow budget

The research notes mostly assumed exactly t points per pile, which forces N = n·t; the user's rule allows any N ≥ n·t, and the difference concentrates in one quantity†. Every final pile holds at least t and the piles together hold all N points, so their combined excess over t is exactly s = N − n·t. Piles never split, so each current pile already at or above t ends inside a single final pile and carries its excess there. Hence **at every moment the committed excess, Σ max(0, size − t) over current piles, cannot exceed s**; while at most n piles have reached t this is equivalent to "the mass outside full piles can still bring every remaining pile up to t," and more than n full piles is possible only if s ≥ t for each surplus pile. With s = 0 the condition reads "no pile ever exceeds t," which is the notes' exact-t world; with s > 0, overshooting becomes a spendable resource. The overfull-pile deadlock disappears, and in its place comes starvation: capsules and snowballs that pour so much into full piles that too little mass remains for the rest. One counter, checked alongside connectivity before each stroke, enforces it.

| Aspect | Notes' exact-t assumption (N = n·t) | User's at-least-t rule (N ≥ n·t) |
|---|---|---|
| Instance arithmetic | N must equal n·t, so the generator needs exact counts | Any N ≥ n·t; slack s/N becomes a difficulty dial, and s = 0 recovers exact t |
| Siting and assignment | Pure transportation problem with every site exactly t | Bradley et al.'s original minimum-size flow with a slack sink of s; still integral |
| Capacity deadlock | Any pile above t is a permanent failure | Excess may total s; the new failure mode is starvation |
| Endgame size repair | Subset-sum search so partial piles sum to exactly t | Counting check (full piles plus ⌊remaining mass ÷ t⌋ ≥ n), plus a tiny bin-covering check once loose points run out |
| Capsule width l | Kept small, or capacity-aware, so captures never overfill | Wider capsules acceptable, limited by bridges and budget |
| Snowballing | Must stop exactly at t | Closes at ≥ t; overshoot spends budget |
| Merging full piles | Forbidden (the result would hold 2t) | Legal, but needs s ≥ t for each surplus full pile |
| Feasibility partition (l ≤ k/2) | n connected groups of exactly t | n connected groups of at least t, a strictly weaker requirement |
| Work lower bound | Lagrangian takes each site's t − 1 cheapest points | Also takes every other negative reduced cost; fixed-site dual weights must be nonnegative |
| Route-first split | Cuts every t points along the tour | Genuine dynamic program over blocks of t to t + s points |
| Pile-size metric | Σ\|size − t\| | Shortfall Σ max(0, t − size) must be zero; total excess is fixed at s, so its spread is descriptive only |
| Local swarm-style rules | Produce wrong sizes, which cannot be repaired | Produce too many piles, which costly merges can repair |

The bound changes are small but must be made, or the benchmark will report invalid gaps†. In the Lagrangian bound L(λ) = Σ_j λ_j + (sum of the n smallest ρ_i), each candidate site's term becomes ρ_i = −λ_i + (sum of the t − 1 smallest values of d_ij − λ_j) + (sum of every remaining negative value of d_ij − λ_j), since a site may now take more than t points. At λ = 0 it coincides with the exact-t bound — the n cheapest "t-nearest-neighbour stars" — and the subproblem stays integral, so the optimized bound equals the LP relaxation of the minimum-size formulation ([Geoffrion](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0120690)). For fixed sites, D(w) = Σ_j min_i (d_ij − w_i) + t·Σ_i w_i remains a certified bound on transport cost, but only for nonnegative weights w, so dual ascent must project onto w ≥ 0. The nearest-neighbour bound and the own-site transport bound are unchanged. On the algorithm side, the minimum-size flow is Bradley et al.'s formulation verbatim ([Bradley, Bennett & Demiriz](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-2000-65.pdf)), the Hilbert seed's equal blocks of ⌊N/n⌋ points already satisfy the minimum, snowballs and wide capsules become competitive where exact t made them reckless, and "grow the largest pile below t" gains a precise meaning: deposits into full piles spend budget, deposits below t do not. Two unknowns remain. The notes surveyed approximation theory only for upper capacities, so the guarantees available for minimum-size clustering are an open question here; and whether slack actually lowers stranding rates and cost, or merely removes a failure mode, is an empirical question the simulator can answer by sweeping s/N upward from zero.

## Conclusion

The most consequential design parameter turns out to be the ratio l/k rather than either value alone. At l ≤ k/2 the static reach graph tells the whole truth — initial feasibility, permanent deadlock and pile-group connectivity all become exact graph questions — which makes the raking game far more tractable than its pushing-puzzle relatives: plays are polynomially long, feasibility sits in NP, and every deadlock is testable in linear time. Above k/2, capsules reach across gaps and the clean picture blurs, so a simulator that wants crisp safety semantics should cap l there or treat wider capsules as a separate game mode. The synthesis also corrected two intuitions the notes left standing: connectivity is not the feasibility frontier, because piles must also be connected groups of at least t, and the MST-based travel bound that looked natural is vacuous on every feasible instance, a gap the area bound closes.

The user's "at least t" choice reframes the problem as a budgeted one, and the slack s/N deserves the same status as clustering strength and k/M in the benchmark design: at zero it reproduces the brittle exact-capacity game, and as it grows the problem relaxes toward a plain n-median with safety constraints, re-legitimizing snowballing, wide capsules and full-pile merges. Beyond that, no literature offers theory for multi-target sweeping with a bounded broom and a reach limit — a gap that recurs across sweeping, coverage and swarm robotics — so a simulator that reports certified gaps on seeded, stratified instances would produce the first systematic evidence on a problem that many fields have circled but none has named.
