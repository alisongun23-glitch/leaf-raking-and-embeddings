# Sub-origins rediscover local PCA, await a semantic test

The proposal is mathematically sound once one sentence is reworded, it is standard mathematics under other names in every geometric step, and its semantic payoff is untested rather than refuted. **Choosing each cluster's centre-most point as a local origin, writing nearby points as vectors in that frame, fencing frames off so they do not overlap, and labelling every point by its nearest cluster is, word for word, the "vector-quantised PCA" of Kambhatla and Leen (1993) and the mixture of local linear models of Tipping and Bishop (1999)**: the centre-most point is a medoid, the frame is a translation (an affine, not linear, map), the fences are Voronoi cells, and the "straight paths" are principal directions. The one statement that needs rewording is the eigenvector one. Being an eigenvector is a property of a matrix, not of a point, so "points on a straight path are eigenvectors" is empty until the matrix is named; the canonical matrix for a line through the origin is the projection uuᵀ, whose 1-eigenspace is exactly that line, and the data-determined directions of a cluster are the eigenvectors of its *mean-centred covariance matrix*, not "of a line". A straight path that misses the origin is not a subspace and is no eigenspace of anything, which is precisely why re-centring must come first. In high dimensions the formulas carry over unchanged but a trap opens: six words in 300 dimensions always span a 5-dimensional flat, so "the cluster has a low-dimensional local frame" is counting, not a finding, and only the shape of the spectrum, judged against random word sets, carries information. On the semantic side the pieces exist separately but nobody has assembled them. Localized centering moves the origin to a local centroid only to fix distances; Rajaee and Pilehvar centre k-means clusters and run local PCA only to delete the top directions, which turned out to encode tense and syntax rather than meaning; Bolukbasi's gender direction is PCA of *pair differences*, not of a word cluster; Park et al. prove that sub-concepts are orthogonal offsets from a parent concept, but only under a whitened inner product in a language model's output layer. **No published work centres a hand-picked semantic field such as the kinship terms at its own mean, runs PCA, and interprets the eigenvectors as meanings.** Kinship is the right test bed because anthropologists already wrote the answer key: sex, generation and lineality. The prediction, from the gender-direction and French distributional literature, is that a local PCA of mixed-sex kin terms will put sex first (a rediscovery), generation second as an ordered axis, lineality a weak third, with word frequency confounded with distance from ego. The experiment costs an afternoon with pretrained GloVe vectors, but it is only meaningful with permutation nulls (an 8-word binary label cannot get below p = 0.029), frequency-matched random word-set controls, leave-one-out stability, and above all a supervised axis built on male terms and tested on held-out female terms.

## Every geometric step has a standard name, and eigenvectors need a matrix

Start with the move that makes everything else possible. A map T is *linear* when T(x + y) = T(x) + T(y) and T(cx) = cT(x); setting c = 0 shows every linear map fixes the origin. Re-centring on a point c is the translation x ↦ x − c, which sends the origin to −c, so it is not linear; it is *affine*, the class of maps y = Ax + b with a linear part A and a shift b, and "translations are affine transformations" that "need not preserve the origin" ([Wikipedia, Affine transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformation)). The eigenvector equation Av = λv says "A stretches the arrow from the origin to v without turning it" ([Wikipedia, Eigenvalues and eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors)), and that arrow changes when the origin moves, so **eigenvectors are only defined after an origin is chosen; the proposal's step 1 must precede its step 2, and the mathematics forces that order.** The same page supplies the proposal's own justification read backwards: "if there is a fixed point, we can take that as the origin, and the affine transformation reduces to a linear transformation" ([Wikipedia, Affine transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformation)). A matrix A applied inside the local frame acts in the original coordinates as x ↦ A(x − c) + c, an affine map whose fixed point is c. Appending a 1 to every vector (homogeneous coordinates) turns the pair (A, b) into a single (d+1)×(d+1) block matrix, and multiplying out its eigenvector equation yields two kinds of eigenvector: pure directions [v; 0] with Av = λv, and fixed points [p; 1] with eigenvalue exactly 1†. A pure translation therefore has every direction as an eigenvector and no fixed point at all: it has eigen-directions but no eigen-points, which is the precise content of the folk statement that "a translation has no eigenvectors". (A dagger marks derivations made by the research team for this report and verified numerically rather than quoted from a source; they deserve independent checking.)

The "centre-most point" has a name and a cost. A *medoid* is "the most centrally located point in the cluster", an actual data point minimising total dissimilarity to the other members, whereas k-means centroids "can fall between data points" ([Wikipedia, k-medoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-medoids)). The cost is that the covariance matrix is defined about the mean: the sample covariance is Q = 1/(N−1) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(xᵢ − x̄)ᵀ, a sum of outer products of the *mean-centred* observations ([Wikipedia, Sample mean and covariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean_and_covariance)), and the population identity K = E[XXᵀ] − μμᵀ ([Wikipedia, Covariance matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance_matrix)) generalises to any other centre c. Writing xᵢ − c = (xᵢ − μ) + (μ − c) and expanding, the cross terms vanish because the centred vectors sum to zero, leaving **S_c = S_μ + (μ − c)(μ − c)ᵀ**†: scatter about the medoid equals scatter about the mean plus a rank-one matrix whose only nonzero eigenvalue is ‖μ − c‖² and whose eigenvector points from the medoid to the mean. The eigenvectors of S_c are therefore *not* those of S_μ unless the medoid happens to lie along a principal axis; centring on the medoid tilts every principal direction toward the medoid-to-mean line, by an amount that grows with ‖μ − c‖² relative to the genuine eigenvalues. The PCA literature's warning that an uncentred rank-one term "often dominates, forcing the leading eigenvector to point almost exactly toward the mean" ([Wikipedia, Principal component analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_component_analysis)) is the same effect with c = 0. On the five-point example (1,1), (2,2.5), (3,2.5), (4,4), (5,5), whose mean is (3,3) and medoid (3,2.5), the leading direction rotates about 2° toward the vertical and the minor eigenvalue more than triples, from 0.049 to 0.174†. So the proposal may use the medoid as the point where arrows start, for display or for "paths into the origin", but should compute the covariance about the mean if it wants standard principal directions; the two answer different questions, "scatter about the representative word" versus "scatter about the centre of mass", and a report should state cos(PC1_medoid, μ − c) so the reader can see how much of a medoid-centred analysis is just that shift.

Now the sentence that needs rewording. For an n×n matrix A and nonzero v, if Av = λv then v is an eigenvector; the eigenvectors for λ are "any nonzero scalar multiples" of one another, and the eigenspace, with the zero vector added, "is a linear subspace" closed under addition and scaling ([Wikipedia, Eigenvalues and eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors)). A line L through the origin with unit direction u is the set of multiples t·u, so if Au = λu then every point of L satisfies A(tu) = λ(tu): all the points on the line are eigenvectors *of A*, with the same eigenvalue. The correct statement is thus not "the points are eigenvectors of the line" but "given a line through the origin, there are matrices whose λ-eigenspace is that line, and the points of the line are eigenvectors of each such matrix". There are infinitely many such matrices, and three are canonical, all built from the outer product uuᵀ. The orthogonal projection P = uuᵀ "leaves u invariant, and it annihilates all vectors orthogonal to u" ([Wikipedia, Projection (linear algebra)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_(linear_algebra))); it is idempotent (P² = P), "the eigenvalues of a projection matrix must be 0 or 1", and its eigenspaces for 1 and 0 are its range and kernel, here L and the orthogonal complement L⊥. The scaling I + (s − 1)uuᵀ stretches by s along L and leaves L⊥ alone; the reflection 2uuᵀ − I keeps L and flips L⊥, the negative of a Householder matrix, which "has eigenvalues ±1" ([Wikipedia, Householder transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Householder_transformation)). **The projection uuᵀ deserves the title "the matrix that encodes the line": it is the unique orthogonal projection whose range is L, it has rank 1, and L is its 1-eigenspace.** A hand-checkable instance: for u = (1,1)/√2, P = [[0.5, 0.5], [0.5, 0.5]], so P(1,1) = (1,1) (eigenvalue 1) while P(−0.5, 0.5) = (0, 0) (eigenvalue 0); a rotation by 30°, by contrast, has no real eigenvectors at all, so the same points are eigenvectors of nothing in particular until a matrix is named. The reverse question is the useful one: *given a matrix that arises from the data*, which directions are its eigenvectors?

A straight path that does not pass through the origin fails outright. A subspace "is a flat in an n-space that passes through the origin" ([Wikipedia, Linear subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_subspace)); the line {p + tu} with p not a multiple of u is not closed under scaling (scaling any of its points by 0 gives the origin, which is not on it), so it is no eigenspace of any linear map, and no single matrix has all its points as eigenvectors with one eigenvalue. Two repairs exist. Translate, so that some point of the line becomes the origin, which is the proposal's step 1 and exactly what makes step 2 meaningful; or treat the points as *fixed points* of the affine orthogonal projection x ↦ Px + (I − P)p onto the line, which in homogeneous coordinates makes them eigenvectors with eigenvalue 1†. The closest true version of the proposal's sentence for a general line is therefore "points on a straight path are the fixed points of the affine projection onto that path".

What a cluster actually determines is its covariance's eigenvectors. Stack the cluster's m points as rows of an m×d matrix X, subtract the mean from every row to get X_c, and form S = X_cᵀX_c/(m − 1). "The principal components are eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix", and the first "maximizes the variance of the projected data" ([Wikipedia, Principal component analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_component_analysis)); Hotelling's definition, as quoted by Tipping and Bishop, is that the principal axes are the dominant eigenvectors of S, with the projected variables uncorrelated and variances equal to the eigenvalues ([Tipping & Bishop 1999](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~blei/seminar/2020-representation/readings/TippingBishop1999.pdf)). For any unit direction w the variance of the cluster's coordinates along w is wᵀSw, and for an eigenvector v_k this equals λ_k: **eigenvalue = variance along its eigenvector**, and the leading eigenvector is the direction of greatest spread. Four points (1,1), (−1,−1), (−0.5,0.5), (0.5,−0.5), with mean at the origin and dividing by m = 4, give S = [[0.625, 0.375], [0.375, 0.625]], eigenvalues 1 and 0.25 with eigenvectors (1,1)/√2 and (1,−1)/√2; projecting the points onto the first gives √2, −√2, 0, 0 with variance exactly 1, and onto the second 0, 0, ∓1/√2 with variance exactly 0.25†. The singular value decomposition X_c = UΣWᵀ gives the same answer more stably: the right singular vectors "are equivalent to the eigenvectors of XᵀX" and the singular values are square roots of its eigenvalues ([Wikipedia, PCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_component_analysis)), so v_k = w_k and λ_k = σ_k²/(m − 1). The coordinates of a point in the cluster's own frame are z = Vᵀ(x − μ), and keeping the first q of them is the local PCA representation, which is also the orthogonal projection minimising squared reconstruction error (Pearson's property, [Tipping & Bishop 1999](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~blei/seminar/2020-representation/readings/TippingBishop1999.pdf)). This is the sense in which the "straight paths" of a cluster are its principal directions: they are the eigenvectors of S, and a candidate path u can be tested by the residual ‖(I − uuᵀ)(x − μ)‖ for each point, zero exactly when the point lies on the line through μ in direction u.

The high-dimensional generalisation is free in the formulas and expensive in the statistics. Nothing above mentions d, so a 300-dimensional cluster is handled identically. But the m centred rows sum to zero, a linear dependency, so **rank(S) ≤ min(m − 1, d)**†: a cluster of 6 words in 300 dimensions has at least 295 eigenvalues that are exactly zero, and the 5 nonzero eigenvectors span exactly the affine hull of the 6 points, which *any* 6 points in general position would also do. Numerically, for 6 random Gaussian points in 300-D the 6×6 Gram matrix X_cX_cᵀ annihilates the all-ones vector to machine precision†. "The cluster lies in a 5-D subspace" is therefore counting, not a finding. The only non-trivial information is the shape of the nonzero spectrum, whether variance concentrates in one or two directions (a path or a thin sheet) or spreads evenly (a blob), and even that must be compared with random clusters of the same size, because random vectors also have unequal eigenvalues by chance. Two further consequences follow. The cluster's own ruler, the Mahalanobis distance d_M(x)² = (x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ), which "is the Euclidean distance after a whitening transformation" and grows "along each principal component axis" in units of that axis's standard deviation ([Wikipedia, Mahalanobis distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalanobis_distance)), needs Σ⁻¹, which does not exist when rank(S) < d; small clusters force a regularised covariance such as probabilistic PCA's WWᵀ + σ²I, where σ² is the average of the discarded eigenvalues ([Tipping & Bishop 1999](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~blei/seminar/2020-representation/readings/TippingBishop1999.pdf)). And "the results of PCA depend on the scaling of the variables" ([Wikipedia, PCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_component_analysis)), so raw and unit-normalised word vectors give different local frames.

The fences and labels are Voronoi cells, and the whole construction is an atlas with the overlaps removed. Assigning each point to its nearest centre partitions space into cells of points "closer to that seed than to any other", convex polytopes used as a subroutine by Lloyd's algorithm, "aka k-means clustering" ([Wikipedia, Voronoi diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voronoi_diagram)); k-means "results in a partitioning of the data space into Voronoi cells" ([Wikipedia, k-means clustering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-means_clustering)). Kambhatla and Leen's VQPCA is the proposal verbatim: "Perform a local PCA within each Voronoi cell of the VQ. For each cell, compute the local covariance matrix for the data with respect to the corresponding reference vector (centroid)", take its eigenvectors, and encode each point by "the index c of the reference cell closest (Euclidean distance) to x, together with the m < n component vector z" of local coordinates ([Kambhatla & Leen 1993](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/1993/file/d554f7bb7be44a7267068a7df88ddd20-Paper.pdf)). Their improvement is worth noting: assigning by "reconstruction distance", the squared distance from a point to a cell's local hyperplane, "directly minimizes the expected reconstruction error" and favours elongated clusters, whereas nearest-centre in each cluster's own Mahalanobis ruler gives quadric rather than flat boundaries†; the proposal should say which "nearest" it means. The probabilistic version is a Gaussian mixture, each component "one local origin μ_k plus one local covariance Σ_k", fitted by EM with soft "partial membership" of every point in every component ([Wikipedia, Mixture model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_model)); k-means is its degenerate case with equal spherical covariances of vanishing variance and hard assignment ([Wikipedia, k-means clustering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-means_clustering)), and Tipping and Bishop's mixture of probabilistic PCA sits between, each component a q-dimensional frame plus isotropic noise with only dq + 1 − q(q − 1)/2 parameters instead of d(d + 1)/2 ([Tipping & Bishop 1999](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~blei/seminar/2020-representation/readings/TippingBishop1999.pdf)). In topology a chart is a coordinate system on an open region and an atlas a family of charts covering the space, with overlapping charts related by transition maps ([Wikipedia, Atlas (topology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_(topology))); the proposal's non-overlapping frames are an atlas whose cells never overlap, hence have no transition maps, so coordinates in different cells cannot be compared. That is exactly the limitation Roweis and Saul attribute to "mixture models for local dimensionality reduction", which "cluster the data and perform PCA within each cluster" but do not map the data "into a single global coordinate system" ([Saul & Roweis, LLE](https://cs.nyu.edu/home/people/in_memoriam/roweis/papers/lle_tr02.pdf)); gluing overlapping patches into one nonlinear coordinate system is what LLE and local tangent space alignment add ([Zhang & Zha 2004](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0212008)). LLE's reconstruction weights, constrained to sum to one so that shifting the origin leaves the reconstruction unchanged, are the general lesson for the proposal: the quantities worth computing in a local frame are the ones that do not depend on which local origin was chosen, namely mean-centred covariance eigenvectors, Mahalanobis distances and reconstruction weights, rather than raw coordinates.

The statement-by-statement verdict collects all of this.

## Prior work has moved origins and read directions, but never both at once

The literature has done each half of the proposal, in different rooms. The origin-shifting half appears in three places, and in every one the local directions are ignored or deleted. Localized centering, by Hara and colleagues, replaces the global centroid with each point's k-nearest-neighbour centroid and subtracts the point's similarity to that local centroid from every similarity score, to reduce "hubness" (a few points appearing in everybody's neighbour lists); it cut the skewness of the neighbour-count distribution on Reuters-52 documents from **14.82 to 1.76** and on 20Newsgroups from 3.25 to 0.81, but the authors state that unlike standard centering it "cannot be interpreted as shifting the origin of the vector space" ([Hara et al. 2015](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/AAAI/2015/localized_centering_reducing_hubness_in_largesample_data__1e4baab2.txt)), and an independent 50-dataset benchmark found it "the weakest method for hubness reduction", with its Euclidean cousin DisSimLocal among the best ([Feldbauer & Flexer 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7327987/)). The local centroid is a nuisance term subtracted from a scalar; nothing is read off directions relative to it.

Rajaee and Pilehvar's cluster-based isotropy correction is the most literal published instance of "move the origin to each cluster's centre and run PCA there". They split BERT, RoBERTa and GPT-2 token vectors into k-means clusters (27, 27 and 10), make each cluster zero-mean, run PCA on each cluster, and null out its top 12 to 30 components ([Rajaee & Pilehvar 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01183)). The geometric payoff is real and quantified: with the whole space zero-meaned, BERT's isotropy score is 0.010, but zero-meaning each of 20 clusters, with no component removal at all, lifts it to **0.262 for BERT and 0.603 for RoBERTa**, direct evidence that these spaces are better described as a union of locally centred blobs than as one blob; the full method lifts STS-B correlation for GPT-2 from 24.8 to 62.3 against 55.7 for the global version ([same source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01183)). They also inspected what the local dominant directions encode, which is the one existing precedent for reading linguistic content off local eigenvectors: in punctuation and stop-word clusters they carry "structural and syntactic information about the sentences", and in verb clusters "tense is more dominant in verb representations than sense-level semantic information", so after removal different-tense synonyms move closer together. The content was read in order to be discarded as noise for similarity tasks. The global ancestor, Mu and Viswanath's all-but-the-top, found that word vectors share a common mean vector with norm roughly one-sixth to one-half of the average word norm and that most energy sits in about 8 of 300 dimensions; subtracting the mean and the top D ≈ d/100 components helps similarity but leaves analogy accuracy almost untouched (word2vec 72.93 to 73.44) because the mean and dominant components cancel in the difference b − a ([Mu, Bhat & Viswanath 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01417)). The lesson of this strand is uncomfortable for the proposal: **the direction of greatest variance inside a set of word vectors is, by default, a nuisance direction (frequency, tense, syntax, position), not a meaning.** Even the question "which global mean?" is unsettled, since for skip-gram embeddings the frequency-weighted mean is the theoretically motivated centre ([Oyama, Yokoi & Shimodaira 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.09663)), and a local-origin proposal inherits the same choice; and later critics note that isotropy "is by definition mean-agnostic" ([Rudman et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.07344)) and that "point clouds cannot both contain well-defined clusters and be isotropic" ([Mickus, Grönroos & Attieh 2024](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.03191)), so whitening clusters away trades classification signal for similarity signal; a frame that keeps the cluster offset as a coordinate would avoid the trade, and no paper does that.

The direction-reading half uses hand-picked pairs, never a cluster's own spread. Bolukbasi and colleagues took ten gender pairs (she–he, mother–father, daughter–son, ...), formed the ten difference vectors, "computed its principal components" and found "a single direction that explains the majority of variance"; "the first eigenvalue is significantly larger than the rest", in contrast to ten random unit vectors in 300-D whose spectrum decays gradually, and in the general algorithm each defining set is centred on its own mean before the SVD ([Bolukbasi et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.06520)). This is the closest existing thing to "an eigenvector encoding a relation", with two differences from the proposal: the centring is per pair, not one origin for a field, and the input is the differences, not the words. It was validated externally, by projecting occupation words and correlating with crowd-rated stereotypes at Spearman 0.51 and across embeddings at 0.81. Gonen and Goldberg then showed the direction measures the concept but does not contain it: after projecting it out, 2-means on the 1,000 most biased words still aligns with gender **92.5%** of the time and a kernel classifier recovers it at 88.9% ([Gonen & Goldberg 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.03862)), so "we found the lineage axis" and "lineage lives on that axis" are different claims. The averaging variants, SemAxis ([An, Kwak & Ahn 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05521)) and the "geometry of culture" dimensions whose gender axis correlates with survey ratings at 0.88 for word2vec ([Kozlowski, Taddy & Evans 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09288)), and semantic projection of category members onto antonym lines, with a median correlation of 0.47 against human ratings and significance in 32 of 52 category-feature pairs, from 0.94 for first names by gender down to −0.15 for cities by cost ([Grand et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10349641/)), all share the recipe: axis from contrasting pairs, validation by held-out projection. Grand's worst cases are the best argument for a per-category frame, since one global axis plainly does not fit every category, but nobody has built one from a category's own eigenvectors.

The one place a "sub-origin" picture has been formalised is in language-model output layers, and it needed a different inner product. Park, Choe and Veitch observe that the unembedding space is defined only up to an invertible affine map, so "nothing in the training objective picks out a natural inner product"; they define a *causal inner product* under which causally separable concepts are orthogonal and show one valid choice is γᵀCov(γ)⁻¹γ′, the inverse covariance of the unembedding vectors ([Park, Choe & Veitch 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658)). Their follow-up proves, in whitened coordinates, that a sub-concept's vector minus its parent's is orthogonal to the parent (mammal − animal ⊥ animal), that sibling differences are orthogonal to the parent, and that a category's members form a simplex, validated on 957 WordNet concepts in Gemma-2B with cosine heatmaps that reproduce the WordNet hierarchy ([Park, Choe, Jiang & Veitch 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01506v3)). That is the proposal's intuition, made exact: sub-concepts are offsets from a parent "origin", and the offsets live in a subspace orthogonal to the parent direction. Three things differ. The directions are estimated from labelled word sets by an LDA-style estimator with shrinkage, not from unsupervised variance; the orthogonality holds only under the whitened inner product, not raw Euclidean geometry; and the authors say it "only works for the final layer representation". Two more results bound what to expect. Sparse-autoencoder work finds millions of near-linear feature directions, far more than the dimension count, in superposition ([Templeton et al. 2024](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/index.html); [Gao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04093)), which implies the top eigenvector of a cluster's covariance is an average over many superposed features and is the reason dictionary learning rather than PCA is used to separate them. And Engels and colleagues found that days of the week and months form *circles* in a 2-D subspace, used by Llama-3-8B to answer 143 of 144 modular-month questions ([Engels et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14860)), so a one-dimensional "lineage axis" is not the only shape a closed set of related words can take.

The geometry without the semantics is thirty years old. Kambhatla and Leen's VQPCA "pieces together local linear coordinate patches" defined by a vector quantiser, with "the two principal eigen-directions in each Voronoi cell", and achieved "significantly lower reconstruction error than the global PCA" on curved data ([Kambhatla & Leen 1993](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/1993/file/d554f7bb7be44a7267068a7df88ddd20-Paper.pdf)); Tipping and Bishop's mixture of probabilistic principal component analysers is "a well-defined mixture model" fitted by EM for "clustering, density modeling, and local dimensionality reduction" ([Tipping & Bishop 1999, Neural Computation](https://mlanthology.org/neco/1999/tipping1999neco-mixtures)). Neither paper is about words. In the embedding world, neighbourhood PCA exists only as tangent-space estimation for manifold learning ([tangent space estimation](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1208.1065)) and local intrinsic-dimension estimation, which reduces each neighbourhood to a scalar: GloVe's intrinsic dimension is about 25 against 130 for random 300-D Gaussians ([Kataiwa et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02142)), and lower-dimension tokens "often form semantically coherent clusters" ([Shared global and local geometry, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.21073)), but no one reports, let alone names, the neighbourhood's principal directions. Evidence that a semantic field is low-dimensional is indirect but real: a word vector sits at cosine **0.72 to 0.76** from the span of its sense vectors, and about 2,000 sparse "discourse atoms" act as directions of semantic fields, each discourse a combination of 3 or 4 ([Arora et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1601.03764)); and rotating GloVe into a basis of per-category dimension weights raises interpretability from 12.0% to 71.3%, with a variant using category *centroids* reaching only 51.5% ([Senel et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.00331)), the only place a category centroid enters this literature, and it underperforms.

So the honest answer to "has this been thought of before" is yes in pieces and no as a whole. What has not been done, after roughly a dozen searches and the papers above, is to take a hand-picked semantic field, centre it at its own mean, run PCA, and interpret the top few eigenvectors as that field's meanings; nor has anyone built per-field anchor representations or a per-field rotation of the kind Densifier learns globally ([Rothe & Schütze 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07572)). Two caveats on that absence claim: the full text of Cai et al.'s ICLR 2021 paper on clusters and manifolds in contextual embedding space could not be opened, and if it interprets within-cluster components the verdict would need revising ([Cai et al. 2021](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2021/poster/3079)); and a forward-citation sweep of Rajaee and Pilehvar was not possible with the tools available. One more body of work frames how to read any result. The offset-analogy literature, from which the whole "relations are directions" picture descends, has been heavily discounted: without excluding the query words the nearest neighbour of a* − a + b is b itself **93%** of the time ([Linzen 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07736)); on the balanced BATS set the offset method solves under 30% of questions, with hypernymy at 4 to 10% ([Gladkova, Drozd & Matsuoka 2016](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002/)); and offsets are parallel only when a co-occurrence statistic is constant across pairs, with accuracy correlating with its variance at r = −0.70, kinship ("family") having one of the highest variances (2.897) despite 0.836 accuracy ([Ethayarajh, Duvenaud & Hirst 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04882)). A single direction encodes a relation only to the extent the statistic is constant; that is a property of pairs, not of a cluster's spread, and it is why the experiment below includes a supervised, pair-based axis as the fair alternative.

## Kinship supplies a three-axis answer key that embeddings will reorder

The reason to test on kin terms rather than, say, fruit is that the answer key was written sixty years ago. Componential analysis, in the form Wallace and Atkins describe, defines each term by its genealogical kin-types and finds the minimal set of feature dimensions whose values the terms signify ([Boster, Categories and Cognitive Anthropology](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)). Two classic analyses of the fifteen core consanguineal (blood-relation) English terms agree on the dimensions and differ on the carving. Wallace and Atkins use sex, absolute generation from +2 to −2, and three degrees of lineality, lineal (direct ancestors or descendants), colineal (siblings and their vertical lines) and ablineal (cousins) ([Borgatti, analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)). Romney and D'Andrade use sex, generation *distance* (0, 1, 2) plus a reciprocity feature (ascending versus descending), and only two degrees of lineality, direct versus collateral, so that reciprocal pairs such as grandfather–grandson and father–son share a feature value instead of sitting at opposite ends of an axis ([Boster](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)). Both are reproduced here from secondary sources that state they adapt the originals' figures; the originals are paywalled.

Brother and sister are colineal for Wallace–Atkins but direct for Romney–D'Andrade; cousin is the sole ablineal term in one and the sole same-generation collateral in the other; its generation row in Wallace–Atkins is unresolved across reproductions.

Which carving is psychologically real was settled by experiment, and the result is a usable discriminator. In triad tests over the eight male terms (pick the "most different" of three), predicted and observed choice frequencies correlated **0.86 and 0.84 with the Romney–D'Andrade model against 0.33 and 0.40 with Wallace–Atkins** in two experiments of 116 and 155 subjects, and even with fitted dimension weights "the Romney-D'Andrade model still fits considerably better" ([analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)); a later judged-similarity study of 122 individuals found a common space accounting for 70 to 90% of the variability ([Romney et al. 1996](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC39342)). The human structure pairs reciprocals and separates direct from collateral; it does not order terms on a signed generation line. That gives a concrete test of *which* model an embedding resembles: under reciprocity father–son should be closer than father–grandfather, under absolute generation they should be equidistant along the generation axis. Kemp and Regier's cross-linguistic analysis adds that the English system is generated by **15 rules** over primitives such as FEMALE, MALE, PARENT and CHILD, and that attested systems in 487 languages, 410 distinct types out of 10⁵⁵ possible, sit near the optimal trade-off between simplicity and informativeness ([Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)); the domain is a compact, almost factorial code, sex 2 × distance 3 × lineality 2 with reciprocity at distance ≥ 1, which is exactly the kind of structure a local PCA could in principle recover as a few near-orthogonal axes. A modern cross-linguistic coding uses six etic features, generation, gender, lineality, gender of connecting relative, relative age and speaker gender ([Hallam et al. 2025](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1fg7b7hn)), of which English marks only the first three.

What learned representations have actually recovered from kinship is the best precedent for the proposal, and it comes from 1986. Hinton trained a small network on 104 relationship facts from two isomorphic family trees, holding out four; the six hidden units encoding each person were never told what features to use, yet "unit 2 encodes which generation a person belongs to", with "the middle generation encoded by an intermediate activity level", so the network "discovers this for itself", and "unit 6 encodes which branch of the family a person belongs to" ([Hinton 1986](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)). Generation and branch, the two non-sex dimensions of the feature table, emerged as separate axes of a distributed representation. Equally instructive is what did not emerge: "none of the 6 units encodes sex", because each relation term already fixes the sex of the second person, so the feature was redundant; an axis absent from an embedding may be absent because something else carries it. Benchmarks, by contrast, test almost nothing of this. The Google analogy set's "family" category is 23 male:female pairs, 506 questions, mixing true kin terms with he:she, king:queen and policeman:policewoman, on which word2vec scores **79.6 ± 1.5** ([questions-words.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nicholas-leonard/word2vec/master/questions-words.txt); [Horn 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02496)); it measures the gender offset, not kinship structure. BATS has no kinship category at all, and its lexicographic relations score 4 to 10% ([Gladkova et al. 2016](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002.pdf)). No published number exists for father→grandfather or father→uncle offsets, so any measurement of the generation or lineality axes is new rather than a replication.

The most relevant distributional study is French. Tanguy and colleagues trained skip-gram models on five corpora and examined 25 kin terms; the cluster is extremely cohesive, **81% of the ten nearest neighbours of each term are themselves kin terms** (92.8% in the combined model), with gender pairs systematically adjacent and groupings for alliance, ascendants and descendants ([Tanguy et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)). Regressing cosine similarity of the 300 pairs on componential feature differences gave adjusted R² from 0.109 (web) to 0.375 (press), every feature significant; gender was the most important feature overall (removing it cost 0.32 of R² in the combined model, 0.50 in Wikipedia), alliance dominated in press and web, ascendance in literature, and descendance and siblinghood were weakest. Polysemy pulls terms out: *fille* ('daughter' and 'girl') has only 41.7% kin neighbours against 95% for *oncle* and sits away from *fils*; *femme* ('wife', 'woman') had to be excluded; and "relatively indirect relations such as cousin, cousine, nièce and neveu tend to sit in median zones or at the intersection of several groupings". Even in a tight, cohesive field, well under half the similarity variance is explained by the classical features, and which feature dominates depends on the corpus.

Put together, these results yield a prediction rather than a hope. **A local PCA of the mixed-sex kinship cluster will almost certainly return sex as its first component**, because gender is the single dominant, near-linear direction in word2vec and GloVe space and kinship pairs are among its purest carriers: daughter→son classifies crowd-sourced gender words at 93%, mother→father at 91%, as well as she→he at 92% ([Bolukbasi et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.06520)), and gender was the strongest feature in the French study. Finding it is a rediscovery of Bolukbasi's direction, not a result. Generation should appear second, as an ordered axis rather than a clean offset, since ordered relations such as hypernymy are exactly where offsets fail yet Hinton's network found generation as a graded unit, and the French ascendant/descendant split was prominent in literary text. Lineality should be a weak third, given that the collateral terms sit in "median zones" and siblinghood was the weakest French feature. This ordering is the *reverse* of the human triads structure, where sex was held constant and generation distance, lineality and reciprocity dominated; the report's framing should be a predicted divergence, not a failure, and the comparison should be made within sex as well as across. Two distortions will overlay all of it. Frequency: everyone has parents but not everyone has children, so "references to parents should tend to be more common" ([Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)), and low-frequency categories score worse on BATS ([Gladkova et al. 2016](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002.pdf)); father, son and brother are common while grandson, nephew and niece are rare, so rarity traces a V-shape in |generation| and splits lineal from collateral unevenly, and a "generation" component may be a frequency component in costume. Polysemy: *grandfather* has a verb sense and grandparents carry a "wisdom" association that "does not reflect a gender difference" ([Bolukbasi et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.06520)); as an unsourced inference, English *father* (priest, Founding Fathers), *son* (Son of God), *brother* and *sister* (religious orders, solidarity, nursing), *uncle* (Uncle Sam) and *cousin* (loose relatedness) will likewise pull those vectors toward religious and institutional neighbourhoods, so the chains with fewest extra senses, grandfather, grandson, nephew, niece, are the cleanest. And every human analysis holds affinal and step terms apart; adding husband, wife or in-laws injects an alliance axis that the fifteen-term key does not contain and that dominated two of the five French corpora.

## An afternoon's experiment, with the safeguards that make it mean something

The computation is an SVD of an 8×300 matrix and takes milliseconds; the design decisions are which words, raw or unit-length vectors, mean or medoid centre, and how to score components against labels without fooling oneself. Vectors come free. gensim's downloader serves GloVe 6B 300-d (400,000 words, **376 MB**, lowercased, public-domain licence), word2vec GoogleNews 300-d (3 million entries, 1,662 MB, cased) and fastText wiki-news subword 300-d (958 MB), cached in ~/gensim-data by `api.load(name)` ([gensim-data](https://github.com/piskvorky/gensim-data)); GloVe can also be fetched raw from the Stanford or Hugging Face mirrors ([stanfordnlp/GloVe](https://github.com/stanfordnlp/GloVe)), and fastText's `.vec` files are plain text with words "sorted by descending frequency" ([fastText English vectors](https://fasttext.cc/docs/en/english-vectors.html)), a fact the frequency controls below exploit. GPT-2's input-embedding matrix, reachable through `get_input_embeddings()` in Hugging Face transformers ([transformers docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/main_classes/model)), is a cheap replicate from a different model family, with the caveat that it is a table of byte-pair *tokens*, "father" and " father" are different rows, and rare words may split into several tokens and must be dropped. GloVe 6B is the natural primary set (smallest, lowercased), GoogleNews the different-algorithm replicate, GPT-2 the different-family replicate; a result that survives all three is worth far more than any one, because nearest-neighbour structure "is highly sensitive to small changes in the training corpus" and single-run results are fragile ([Antoniak & Mimno 2018](https://aclanthology.org/Q18-1008/)).

Step zero, before looking at a single vector, is to fix the words and the labels. Pre-registration is the cheapest safeguard there is. A single-sex set keeps the sex direction out of the picture: the six words of the original proposal plus grandson and cousin, with two pre-registered labels.

| word | generation relative to ego | lineal (1) / collateral (0) |
|---|---|---|
| grandfather | +2 | 1 |
| father | +1 | 1 |
| son | −1 | 1 |
| grandson | −2 | 1 |
| uncle | +1 | 0 |
| brother | 0 | 0 |
| nephew | −1 | 0 |
| cousin | 0 | 0 |

The female mirror set (grandmother, mother, daughter, granddaughter, aunt, sister, niece, cousin) is held out for the transfer test, and combining both gives a fifteen-word set with sex as a third label. Then fetch the vectors into X (m × d) and decide on normalisation: raw vectors differ in length as well as direction, and in static embeddings length is tied to frequency and to the common component whose norm is up to half the average norm ([Mu & Viswanath 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01417v2)), so PCA on raw vectors mixes length variation with angular variation while PCA on unit vectors sees only angles, which is what cosine-based reasoning about meaning assumes; run both, and if a component survives only on raw vectors, suspect frequency. Choose the centre, mean or medoid, remembering that the medoid-centred scatter equals the mean-centred scatter plus m(μ − c)(μ − c)ᵀ†, so if the medoid is far from the mean the medoid analysis's first component will tend to be "where the medoid word sits relative to the rest", a fact about one word; report mean-centred components as primary and cos(PC1_medoid, μ − c) alongside. Centre, take the SVD X_c = USVᵀ, and read off the rows of Vᵀ as directions in R³⁰⁰, US as the words' coordinates on each component, s_k²/(m − 1) as the eigenvalues and s_k²/Σs² as explained-variance ratios. Plot PC1 against PC2 with those ratios printed on the axes. Score each component against each label: Spearman's rank correlation for the ordinal generation label, point-biserial correlation (Pearson r against a 0/1 label) for the binary ones, and never report a correlation without the nulls that follow.

The degrees-of-freedom problem is the first null, and it is exact. After mean-centring the m rows sum to zero, so rank(X_c) ≤ m − 1, and the non-trivial component scores span the whole (m − 1)-dimensional space of mean-zero vectors in R^m. Any centred label vector, generation, lineality, sex, or a labelling drawn from a hat, lives in that same space, so there is always a unit direction w with X_c w = y exactly: **every labelling of eight words is perfectly encoded by some direction in their span**†. "A direction exists" is vacuous. The only non-vacuous claims are that a labelling aligns with a *top* component, a claim about variance ordering testable without labels, or that a direction built from some words predicts the labels of *other* words. For comparison, eight random isotropic points in R³⁰⁰ give seven nearly equal eigenvalues of about 14% each, so an explained-variance ratio of 25% for PC1 is not automatically meaningful†.

The permutation test comes next, and its resolution is brutal at this size. Components are computed without labels, so the score vector is fixed and the null is "labels are exchangeable": shuffle labels, recompute the statistic, count. With eight words and a 4/4 binary label there are C(8,4) = 70 distinct labellings, so **the smallest attainable two-sided p-value is 2/70 ≈ 0.029**; the generation multiset {−2, −1, −1, 0, 0, +1, +1, +2} admits 8!/(2!·2!·2!) = 5,040 distinct labellings; the fifteen-word set with a 7/8 sex split gives C(15,7) = 6,435†. For m ≤ 9 enumerate every distinct labelling exactly; for larger m draw M random shuffles and report p = (b + 1)/(M + 1), never b/M, because randomly drawn permutation p-values are otherwise "understated by about 1/m" ([Phipson & Smyth 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05766)). Use |ρ| unless the sign was pre-registered, and if you test "the best of PC1 to PC3", make the permutation statistic the *maximum over the three components*, so the search is inside the null and the multiple-component comparison corrects itself. The tempting design of 3 components × 3 labels × 2 centrings × 2 normalisations is 36 tests needing Bonferroni α = 0.05/36 = 0.0014, which the eight-word set cannot reach for any binary label and only barely for generation; either pre-register one primary test ("PC1 of the mean-centred, unit-normalised male set correlates with generation") and call the rest exploratory, or fold the search into the max-statistic.

Random word-set controls answer "is this cluster special?" rather than "is this labelling special?". The explained-variance ratio of PC1 and the best |ρ| over components are both inflated for tiny m regardless of meaning, so build the null empirically: draw 1,000 sets of m words, run the identical pipeline with the identical label vector assigned in arbitrary order, and record where the kinship set falls in the distribution of EVR₁ and of max|ρ|. Match frequency, because high- and low-frequency words "lie in different subregions of the embedding space" ([Gong et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06858v2)): the GloVe and fastText files are frequency-ordered, so take each kinship word's rank r and draw its control from the band [r/2, 2r]. Bolukbasi's own version of this control, the spectrum of ten random unit vectors averaged over 1,000 draws, is the precedent ([Bolukbasi et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.06520)). Leave-one-out stability catches the opposite failure, axes that belong to the word list rather than the space: with m = 8 each word is 12.5% of the data, so re-run dropping each word in turn (and adding plausible extras: cousin, great-grandfather, stepfather, father-in-law), record |cos(PC1_drop-i, PC1_full)|, and treat any drop below about 0.9 or any PC1/PC2 swap as disqualifying; report the eigengap (λ₁ − λ₂)/λ₁, since nearly equal eigenvalues leave the two eigenvectors defined only up to rotation within their plane and then only the *subspace* should be compared.

The decisive test is transfer to held-out words, and it rescues the proposal from the variance-ordering question. Unsupervised PCA asks "is lineage the *largest* source of variance among these eight words?", which can be false even when a perfectly good lineage direction exists at PC3 or spread over several components. The supervised alternative asks the actual question, in the style of Bolukbasi, Kozlowski and Grand: build "generation" from labelled pairs, the unit differences father − son, grandfather − father, grandfather − grandson, uncle − nephew, as their mean or as the top component of the difference matrix; build "lineal versus collateral" as mean(lineal words) − mean(collateral words); then project the female words, which played no part in the construction, and compute Spearman against their generation labels and point-biserial against lineality. Two nulls: permute the held-out labels, and, stronger, project the held-out words onto 10,000 random unit directions in R³⁰⁰ and ask how often a random direction does as well. Because sex is itself a strong direction, a male-built axis that transfers to female words is evidence that the generation direction is roughly *parallel* across sexes, which is the parallelogram claim people make about kin terms, now tested on words the axis never saw; then reverse the roles. The cleanest unsupervised statistic for the "straight path" intuition is offset collinearity along a chain: cos(father − son, grandfather − father), its female mirror, and the cross-sex cos(father − son, mother − daughter), against an empirical null of cosines between offsets of random vocabulary pairs. For isotropic random directions in 300-D the cosine would have standard deviation about 1/√300 ≈ 0.058, but real embeddings are anisotropic, so the empirical null will be wider and shifted; use it, not the formula. Any analogy quoted alongside, such as father : son :: mother : daughter, must exclude the three query words and must come with Linzen's ONLY-B baseline, whether "daughter" already has "mother" as its nearest neighbour, since without exclusion the offset method returns b itself 93% of the time and ONLY-B alone reaches 0.70 on plurals ([Linzen 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07736)). Across models the directions cannot be compared directly, since each space has its own axes, so compare the *score vectors*, the eight numbers per model, by Spearman correlation across GloVe, word2vec, fastText and GPT-2.

The core test is about forty lines of numpy, with the exact-enumeration permutation test in three of them; the skeleton was written against documented APIs but not executed in the research session, so smoke-test it before trusting numbers.

```python
import itertools, numpy as np
import gensim.downloader as api
from scipy.stats import spearmanr

kv = api.load("glove-wiki-gigaword-300")                      # 376 MB, cached in ~/gensim-data
words = ["grandfather","father","son","grandson","uncle","brother","nephew","cousin"]
gen   = np.array([ 2, 1,-1,-2, 1, 0,-1, 0])                    # generation relative to ego (pre-registered)
lin   = np.array([ 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0])                    # 1 = lineal, 0 = collateral
X = np.stack([kv[w] for w in words]).astype(np.float64)
X /= np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)                  # unit-normalise; comment out to test raw vectors

def pca(X, centre):
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X - centre, full_matrices=False)  # rows of Vt = principal directions in R^300
    return U * s, Vt, s**2 / np.sum(s**2)                      # scores (m x r), directions, explained-variance ratio

def perm_p(x, lab, M=20000, rng=np.random.default_rng(0)):     # two-sided permutation p for |Spearman rho|
    r = abs(spearmanr(x, lab).correlation)
    if len(lab) <= 9:                                          # small m: enumerate every distinct labelling exactly
        null = np.array([abs(spearmanr(x, p).correlation) for p in set(itertools.permutations(lab))])
        return r, (null >= r).mean(), len(null)
    null = np.array([abs(spearmanr(x, rng.permutation(lab)).correlation) for _ in range(M)])
    return r, ((null >= r).sum() + 1) / (M + 1), M              # Phipson & Smyth (2010) +1 correction

D = np.linalg.norm(X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :], axis=2)      # pairwise distances
j = int(np.argmin(D.sum(1))); print("medoid word:", words[j])
for name, c in [("mean", X.mean(0)), ("medoid", X[j])]:
    scores, Vt, evr = pca(X, c)
    print(f"\ncentre = {name:6s}  explained variance (PC1..PC4): {np.round(evr[:4], 3)}")
    if name == "medoid":
        print("  cos(PC1_medoid, mean - medoid) =", round(abs(Vt[0] @ (X.mean(0) - X[j]) / np.linalg.norm(X.mean(0) - X[j])), 3))
    for k in range(3):
        for lname, lab in [("generation", gen), ("lineal", lin)]:
            r, p, n = perm_p(scores[:, k], lab)
            print(f"  PC{k+1} vs {lname:10s} |rho| = {r:.2f}   exact p = {p:.3f}   (distinct labelings = {n})")
```

    print(f"drop {w:12s}  |cos(PC1, PC1_full)| = {abs(Vt_i[0] @ Vt_full[0]):.2f}")

Frequency-matched random-set control for EVR₁ and for the best |ρ| over PC1 to PC3 (gensim's `index_to_key` is in file order, descending frequency, for these sets):

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1); ranks = [kv.key_to_index[w] for w in words]
best, evr1 = [], []
for _ in range(1000):
    pick = [kv.index_to_key[rng.integers(max(1, r // 2), 2 * r + 2)] for r in ranks]   # same rank band as each kinship word
    R = np.stack([kv[w] for w in pick]).astype(float); R /= np.linalg.norm(R, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    sc, _, ev = pca(R, R.mean(0)); evr1.append(ev[0])
    best.append(max(abs(spearmanr(sc[:, k], gen).correlation) for k in range(3)))
print("random sets: 95th pct of max|rho| =", np.percentile(best, 95), "  95th pct of EVR1 =", np.percentile(evr1, 95))
```

Supervised axis with held-out transfer, Bolukbasi-style PCA of unit differences built on male terms and tested on female terms against a random-direction null:

```python
male   = ["father","son","grandfather","grandson","uncle","nephew"]; female = ["mother","daughter","grandmother","granddaughter","aunt","niece"]
g = np.array([1,-1,2,-2,1,-1])
M = np.stack([kv[w] for w in male]).astype(float); F = np.stack([kv[w] for w in female]).astype(float)
diffs = np.stack([M[0]-M[1], M[2]-M[0], M[2]-M[3], M[4]-M[5]]); diffs /= np.linalg.norm(diffs, axis=1, keepdims=True)
axis = np.linalg.svd(diffs, full_matrices=False)[2][0]; axis *= np.sign(axis @ (M[0] - M[1]))   # orient: older > younger
obs = spearmanr(F @ axis, g).correlation
Rd = rng.standard_normal((10000, F.shape[1])); Rd /= np.linalg.norm(Rd, axis=1, keepdims=True)
null = np.abs([spearmanr(F @ d, g).correlation for d in Rd])
print(f"held-out female rho = {obs:+.2f};  P(|rho_random dir| >= |obs|) = {((null >= abs(obs)).sum() + 1) / (len(null) + 1):.4f}")
```

For the GPT-2 replicate, replace the loading block with `tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2"); m = AutoModel.from_pretrained("gpt2"); E = m.get_input_embeddings().weight.detach().numpy()`, keep a word only if `len(tok.encode(" " + w)) == 1`, and take `X = E[[tok.encode(" "+w)[0] for w in words]]`; everything else is unchanged. A dependency-free Node version, closer to the raking simulator's own language, rests on one trick that is worth knowing in its own right: for centred X_c the m×m Gram matrix G = X_cX_cᵀ has the same nonzero eigenvalues as the 300×300 covariance, and if Gu_k = λ_k u_k with ‖u_k‖ = 1 then the words' PC-k scores are √λ_k·u_k and the direction in R³⁰⁰ is X_cᵀu_k/√λ_k†, so one only ever diagonalises an 8×8 symmetric matrix, for which a thirty-line Jacobi rotation routine suffices.

```js
// node kinship.js glove.6B.300d.txt
const fs = require("fs"), readline = require("readline");
const words = ["grandfather","father","son","grandson","uncle","brother","nephew","cousin"];
const gen = [2,1,-1,-2,1,0,-1,0], want = new Set(words), vec = {};
const rl = readline.createInterface({ input: fs.createReadStream(process.argv[2]) });
rl.on("line", line => { const i = line.indexOf(" "), w = line.slice(0, i);
  if (want.has(w)) vec[w] = line.slice(i + 1).trim().split(" ").map(Number); });
rl.on("close", () => {
  const X = words.map(w => vec[w].map((v, _, r) => v / Math.hypot(...r)));      // unit-normalise
  const m = X.length, d = X[0].length;
  const mean = Array.from({ length: d }, (_, j) => X.reduce((s, r) => s + r[j], 0) / m);
  const Xc = X.map(r => r.map((v, j) => v - mean[j]));
  const G = Xc.map(a => Xc.map(b => a.reduce((s, v, j) => s + v * b[j], 0)));   // m x m Gram matrix
  const { values, vectors } = jacobiEigen(G);                                    // symmetric 8x8 -> your own Jacobi routine
  // scores on PC k: values[k]**0.5 * vectors[k];  explained variance: values[k] / sum(values)
  // then: Spearman(scores_k, gen) and an exact permutation test over the distinct arrangements of gen
});
```

Streaming a 1 GB GloVe text file through `readline` takes on the order of a minute; the exact enumeration of 5,040 generation labellings is a trivial loop; and for the random-set control keep the first 50,000 lines in memory as `Float32Array`s (about 60 MB). If the basic test works, the natural extensions are transfer in both directions across sex, replication across the four vector sets, a "neutralise-then-look" pass that projects out the sex direction and the top two or three global components before re-running the unsupervised PCA (if generation then surfaces as PC1, the earlier result was masking, not absence), and the same recipe on other small fields: days of the week and months, where a ring in PC1–PC2 rather than an ordinal axis is the prediction from Engels et al., number words, colour terms, and military ranks, a clean ordinal with a frequency confound much like kinship's.

## Ten ways a small-cluster PCA fools its author

Most of these have appeared above in passing; collected, with the diagnostic for each, they are the checklist to run before believing any axis.

| Pitfall | Why it bites here | Diagnostic or fix |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency is a dominant direction | High- and low-frequency words occupy different subregions ([Gong et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06858v2)); father/son/brother are common, grandson/nephew rare, so rarity tracks \|generation\| | Report Spearman(PC_k, log frequency rank) next to Spearman(PC_k, generation); compare raw against unit-normalised runs; frequency-matched random sets |
| Global mean and the top global components | Vectors share a common vector of up to half the average norm and a few dominant directions ([Mu & Viswanath 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01417v2)); local centring removes the mean but the top global directions remain inside the cluster's scatter | Run twice, as shipped and after subtracting the global mean over the top 50k words and projecting out the top 2–3 global components; a component that disappears was a global direction in a kinship costume |
| Hubness, and the medoid as a hub | Hubs "tend to be among the k nearest neighbors of a large number of data items" and hubness "is an inherent property" of high-dimensional data ([Radovanović et al. 2010](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/radovanovic10a.html); [Schnitzer et al. 2012](https://jmlr.org/papers/v13/schnitzer12a.html)); distances concentrate, so the medoid choice is decided by small margins and the "centre-most" word is liable to be the most generic, frequent member for reasons unrelated to kinship | Re-check the medoid after dropping any one word; compute each kinship word's k-occurrence among a 50k-word subset and compare with that subset's distribution |
| Polysemy | Senses sit "in linear superposition within the word embedding" ([Arora et al. 2018](https://aclanthology.org/Q18-1034/)), so father − son mixes "male parent minus male child" with "priest minus religious Son"; lowercased GloVe merges Father/father | Prefer chains with few extra senses (grandfather, grandson, nephew, niece); use the cased GoogleNews set as a check |
| Sex dominates any mixed-sex set | Daughter–son and mother–father are two of Bolukbasi's ten gender-defining pairs ([Bolukbasi et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.06520)) | Single-sex sets, or neutralise first: x ← x − (x·g)g, then look for generation and lineage in what remains |
| Sensitivity to word list and training run | One word is 12.5% of an 8-word set, so adding "cousin" or "mother" can swap PC1 and PC2; neighbour structure is "highly sensitive to small changes in the training corpus" ([Antoniak & Mimno 2018](https://aclanthology.org/Q18-1008/)) | Leave-one-out cosines; replicate across GloVe, word2vec, fastText and GPT-2 and believe only what survives |
| Eigenvector sign and rotation | Eigenvectors are defined up to sign; nearly equal eigenvalues leave a pair defined only up to rotation in their plane | Fix a sign convention (orient PC1 so grandfather scores positive); report the eigengap; compare subspaces by principal angles or canonical correlation when the gap is small |
| "Points on a straight line into the origin are eigenvectors" | A category error: eigenvectors belong to a square matrix, not to a point cloud. If *uncentred* vectors are collinear through the origin, XᵀX has rank one with that line as its sole eigenvector, but PCA uses the *centred* scatter, whose eigenvectors describe spread about the mean and bear no special relation to the origin | Test the intuition as collinearity of *offsets*: cos(grandfather − father, father − son) near 1, with the empirical random-pair null |
| 2-D plots hide variance | With seven nonzero eigenvalues from eight words, PC1 + PC2 may explain only 40–60% of the scatter, and eight random points in R³⁰⁰ already give ≈14% per component | Print explained-variance ratios on the axes; show the random-set distribution of EVR₁ |
| Analogy quotes without baselines | Without excluding query words the offset method returns b itself 93% of the time; ONLY-B alone reaches 0.70 on plurals ([Linzen 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07736)) | Exclude the three query words and report ONLY-B and IGNORE-A alongside any analogy |

Two more belong on the list though they are structural rather than empirical. The mean-versus-medoid rank-one shift means a medoid-centred PC1 can be a fact about one word; and the rank ≤ m − 1 tautology means a direction for any labelling always exists, which is why the random-set and held-out tests are not optional. A last caution is specific to embedding spaces: cosines and angles are measured from the global origin, and translating to a local origin changes every angle, so "similarity" inside a local frame is a different quantity from similarity in the global frame; the origin-invariant quantities, centred covariance eigenvectors, Mahalanobis distances and LLE-style reconstruction weights, are the safe ones to report.

## Conclusion

The proposal is a correct re-description of local PCA with Voronoi assignment, and that is worth more than it sounds: the learner has independently reconstructed, from a two-dimensional raking game, the architecture of vector-quantised PCA, mixtures of probabilistic principal component analysers, and the parent-origin-plus-orthogonal-offsets picture that Park et al. proved for language-model concepts. The one correction, that eigenvectors belong to the cluster's mean-centred covariance (or to a named matrix such as the projection uuᵀ) and never to "a line", is the correction that turns the idea into something computable. What remains genuinely open is the semantics, and the literature's consistent experience is that a cluster's largest direction of variance is sex, frequency, tense or syntax rather than the meaning one hoped for, that a direction which *measures* a concept rarely *contains* it, that in modern models the Euclidean metric is the wrong one until the space is whitened, and that some closed fields are circles rather than lines. The implication for the experiment is to treat unsupervised PCA as the exploratory step and the supervised, pair-built axis with held-out transfer as the real test; a generation axis built on male terms that orders the female terms correctly, against permutation and random-direction nulls, in three independently trained spaces, would be a new result, since no published number exists for any kinship axis other than sex.

The bridge back to the raking game is exact rather than metaphorical. Assigning every point to its nearest pile is the Voronoi partition that k-means uses as a subroutine; siting a pile on an actual point rather than at a centre of mass is the k-medoids choice, with the medoid under plain distance and "the point nearest the centroid" under squared distance being different points that coincide only sometimes†; and the k-means objective, the within-cluster sum of squared distances, equals Σ_k m_k·tr(S_k), that is, each pile's size times the sum of its local PCA eigenvalues†, so k-means is literally choosing the partition whose local spectra have the smallest size-weighted total, which can also be written as pairwise squared deviations without naming any centre ([Wikipedia, k-means clustering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-means_clustering)). The rake pulling points along straight lines into a pile is a stroke along a direction u, and the local PCA of a pile is the answer to "which direction would a single straight stroke serve best": a pile whose spectrum is one large eigenvalue and the rest small is a path, raked in one line, while a round spectrum is a blob that needs spokes. Soft assignment, the Gaussian-mixture generalisation in which a point belongs to every pile with a weight, is the version of the game where a leaf halfway between two piles is half-raked toward each, and k-means is the limit in which every pile is forced to use the same round ruler. Running the kinship experiment is, in that language, playing the raking game in 300 dimensions with words as leaves, with the difference that nobody yet knows whether the straightest stroke through a pile of kin terms means anything.
