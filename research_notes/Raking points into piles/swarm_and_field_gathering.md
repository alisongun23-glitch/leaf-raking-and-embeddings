## Q1. Ant-based clustering and sorting (Deneubourg et al. 1991; Lumer & Faieta 1994): pick-up and drop rules, how the pile count changes over time, and what leads to one pile or many

### Takeaway
Clustering emerges from two opposed, density-dependent rules: pick up where items are sparse, and drop where they are dense. The pile count follows a characteristic **rise, peak, then slow coarsening** curve. Real ants (Theraulaz et al. 2002) show the mechanism is **local activation, long-range inhibition**. This gives a natural pile spacing, a pile count that scales with density × area, and a critical density below which no piles form. Many small piles merge only slowly into a few large ones, and naive data-clustering versions (Lumer–Faieta) end with **too many clusters**. The fixes that help are memory of where earlier drops went, mixing coarse and fine movers, and a second pass that treats piles as single objects.

### Cited Findings
**Deneubourg et al. (1991) "basic model" (BM)**
- Citation: Deneubourg, Goss, Franks, Sendova-Franks, Detrain & Chrétien, "The dynamics of collective sorting: robot-like ants and ant-like robots," in Meyer & Wilson (eds.), *From Animals to Animats* (SAB 1990 proceedings), MIT Press, 1991, pp. 356–363 or 356–365 (the two listings disagree) [PR, conference] — [search record](https://typeset.io/authors/l-chretien-t2wtftcded); [Ramos & Merelo 2002](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075)
- Rules: an unladen agent picks up an item with P_p = (k1/(k1+f))², and a laden agent drops it with P_d = (f/(k2+f))². Here f is the perceived fraction of items nearby. It is computed from **short-term memory**: items met in the last T time steps divided by the maximum possible, so f = N/T if at most one item can be met per step. When f ≪ k1, P_p ≈ 1, so isolated items get picked up. When f ≫ k2, P_d ≈ 1, so items get dropped in dense spots [2ND] — [Ramos & Merelo, AEB'02 / arXiv cs/0412075](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075)
- Parameters reported for the simulations: k1 = 0.1, k2 = 0.3. The sorting run used 400 items of 2 types on a 100×100 grid, with 10 agents and T = 50. About 5,000,000 iterations were needed for a good visual result [2ND] — [Ramos & Merelo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075)
- Dynamics: small, evenly spaced clusters appear fairly quickly, then **merge more slowly into fewer, larger clusters** [2ND] — [Ramos & Merelo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075)
- Robot framing: the agents move randomly, do not communicate, have no global representation and perceive only objects directly in front of them [2ND, SNIPPET] — [York SAB index page](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~ss44/books/pages/s/ASendovaFranks.htm)
- Biological caveat: later experimental work suggests ants use brood density to decide when to *pick up* an item but not when to *put it down*, which differs from the BM's use of density for both [PR, 2ND] — [Melhuish et al. 2006, J R Soc Interface](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1578749/)

**Lumer & Faieta (1994) (LF): from corpse clustering to data clustering**
- Neighbourhood "density" with a dissimilarity scale α: f(o_i) = max(0, (1/s²) Σ_{o_j in s×s neighbourhood} [1 − d(o_i,o_j)/α]). Pick-up is P_p = (k1/(k1+f))². Drop is **piecewise**: P_d = 2f if f < k2, and 1 otherwise. Parameters: k1 = 0.1, **k2 = 0.15 (BM used 0.3)**, α = 0.5, t_max = 10⁶ steps. The demonstration used 4 Gaussian clusters of 200 points each on a 100×100 non-toroidal grid with 10 ants [2ND; equation layout garbled in extraction, but all terms present] — [Ramos & Merelo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075)
- **Over-fragmentation**: the projected map typically has *more* clusters than the true data. LF added three fixes. (1) **Ant speeds spread over [1, v_max = 6]**: fast ants are less selective and place items roughly in the right region, while slow ants refine at small scale (multi-scale clustering). (2) **Short-term memory** of the last m drops: an ant carrying a new item heads for the remembered location of the most similar item instead of walking randomly, which reduces duplicate clusters. (3) **Behavioural switches**: ants that have not picked up or dropped anything for a set number of steps start destroying clusters, a kind of annealing that escapes poor configurations [2ND] — [Ramos & Merelo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075)
- An independent assessment also says LF typically produces too many clusters and converges slowly [PR, conference; the PDF is a World Scientific proceedings draft dated April 2004, probably FLINS 2004, venue unverified] — [Schockaert, De Cock, Cornelis & Kerre 2004, "Efficient clustering with fuzzy ants"](https://faculty.washington.edu/mdecock/papers/sschockaert2004.pdf)
- **Monmarché's heap-based variant (AntClass)**, as summarized by Schockaert et al.: several items may share a cell (a heap), and an ant with capacity c can carry up to c items. It runs in **two passes**. Pass 1 uses capacity 1 and produces many tight clusters. Pass 2 treats the pass-1 heaps as atomic objects and uses unlimited capacity, so whole heaps get merged. k-means cleans up after each pass [2ND] — [Schockaert et al. 2004](https://faculty.washington.edu/mdecock/papers/sschockaert2004.pdf)
- Schockaert et al. use a **single** ant that can pick up either one item from a heap or the **entire heap**. The choice follows response-threshold rules of the form T(s;θ) = sⁿ/(sⁿ+θⁿ) [PR, conference] — [Schockaert et al. 2004](https://faculty.washington.edu/mdecock/papers/sschockaert2004.pdf)

**Real ants: Theraulaz et al. (2002), cemetery formation in *Messor sancta***
- Citation: Theraulaz, Bonabeau, Nicolis, Solé, Fourcassié, Blanco, Fournier, Joly, Fernández, Grimal, Dalle & Deneubourg, "Spatial patterns in ant colonies," *PNAS* 99(15):9645–9649 (2002), doi:10.1073/pnas.152302199 [PR] — [PNAS paper copy](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)
- Workers pick up corpses and drop them into piles; several clusters exist within a few hours. **The number of clusters rises to a maximum after about 3 h, then falls and stabilizes**. Some clusters grow while others disappear, and surviving clusters grow along a **sigmoid** curve, which points to autocatalysis. A pile is defined as at least 5 corpses [PR] — [Theraulaz et al. 2002](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)
- Measured rules: unladen ants pick up with a probability that **falls with cluster size**, and laden ants drop with a probability that **rises with cluster size**. Laden ants also drop spontaneously: the log of the proportion still carrying falls linearly with distance walked, with k_d = 0.75 m⁻¹ (r² = 0.975). Ants random-walk along the arena edge at 1.6 ± 0.7 cm/s, make U-turns at 0.10 s⁻¹ (mean free path 15.8 cm), and perceive corpses within about 0.5–1.0 cm [PR] — [Theraulaz et al. 2002](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)
- Model: a reaction–diffusion **activator–substrate (LALI)** system. Adding corpses becomes more likely as a cluster grows (local self-enhancement), while the depletion of corpses around a cluster inhibits nearby clusters. The fitted dropping and picking constants are 31.75, 1,000, 3.125 and 50 m⁻¹ [PR] — [Theraulaz et al. 2002](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)
- Linear stability analysis predicts, and experiments confirm, three things for the early phase. (i) **Doubling corpse density doubles the number of piles**. (ii) **Doubling the arena diameter at constant density doubles the number of piles**. (iii) There is a **critical density (c_c ≈ 46 corpses/m)** below which no aggregation occurs; at 13 corpses/m no stable clusters formed. Experiments used Ø 25 cm and Ø 50 cm arenas (edge lengths 78.5 and 157.1 cm) with 100–400 corpses [PR] — [Theraulaz et al. 2002](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)
- Late phase: there is a **critical distance between neighbouring piles below which only one survives**. In the small arena after 24 h, two piles within 20 cm of each other were very unlikely, and piles were never closer than 10 cm. The most common outcome, seen in 50% of cases, was two piles on **opposite sides** of the arena, a distribution significantly different from random [PR] — [Theraulaz et al. 2002](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf)

**Minimal models**
- Martin, Chopard & Albuquerque (2002), *Future Generation Computer Systems* 18(7):951–959, doi:10.1016/S0167-739X(02)00074-2. A minimal cellular-automaton ant, simpler than Deneubourg's, still produces cemeteries. The authors argue that corpse clustering is a **statistical effect**, that **a single ant would produce it too, only more slowly**, and that clusters tend to form around environmental inhomogeneities [PR; abstract only] — [UNIGE archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:120656)
- Sakiyama (2020), *Scientific Reports* 10:2344, doi:10.1038/s41598-020-59202-0. A 1-D lattice of 100 sites with 100 agents and 400 corpses; a pile is at least 5 corpses; pick-up probability is 0.05; drop probability is 0.5 or 0.1 around a threshold of 10. **Many piles form early and then disappear**, and after a peak some grow while others vanish, matching experiments. Halving the density, or halving the field length at constant density, roughly **halved the number of piles**. A gradual rise in drop probability with cluster size matched experiments better than a step threshold [PR] — [PMC7012894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7012894/)
- Textbook synthesis: Bonabeau, Dorigo & Theraulaz, *Swarm Intelligence: From Natural to Artificial Systems* (Oxford UP, 1999) covers the Deneubourg and LF models [BOOK; chapter not read here] — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/40811/chapter/348771865)

### Inferences
- **What sets pile count in local-rule systems:** the count is set by the *interaction range* (perception radius and carry distance), not by any target. Theraulaz's result that the most unstable wavenumber is proportional to density implies **pile count ∝ density × area** for a fixed rule. A local rule in our game therefore fixes the pile count at roughly (area)/(reach area), whatever n = N/t is. This explains LF over-fragmentation and predicts that **purely local greedy heuristics will produce the wrong number of piles unless the reach k and capsule width l happen to suit t**.
- **Useful dimensionless groups for the game** (my construction from the above): expected points within reach, D_k = λπk², and expected capture per unit stroke length, c_l ≈ 2lλ, where λ is local point density. When D_k ≈ t, local seeding naturally yields piles of about t. When D_k ≪ t, piles need multi-hop transport and merging chains. When D_k ≫ t, sweeps can capture more than one pile's worth and **overfill** becomes the main risk. A rough analogue of Theraulaz's critical density is the regime where nearest-neighbour gaps approach k: below it, local pile growth "does not nucleate", and the k-graph may already be disconnected.
- **Coarsening is slow because equal piles stall.** Equal-sized piles far apart (the "opposite sides" outcome) are metastable. For a single deliberate agent, the lesson is to **merge piles while they are still close and unequal**, and not leave two half-piles far apart for the endgame.
- **Memory beats random search.** LF's short-term memory, which takes an item to the remembered best site, removes duplicate clusters. The single-agent version is to **keep an explicit list of target pile sites** and always deliver to a planned site rather than founding piles opportunistically.
- **Two passes beat one** (Monmarché). First form many tight micro-piles cheaply, then merge whole piles. This maps directly onto the game's "drag a whole pile" and "merge piles within k" operations.
- Pick-up falling and drop rising with pile size is a soft "**grow the largest**" rule. With an exact-t requirement it must be **capped**: a pile that reaches t should become inert, unlike ant piles, which grow without limit.

### Gaps
- I could not open Deneubourg et al. (1991) or Lumer & Faieta (1994) themselves. Formulas and parameter values come from Ramos & Merelo (2002) and Schockaert et al. (2004). LF's venue (*From Animals to Animats 3*, SAB'94, MIT Press, often cited as pp. 501–508) is from memory and **unverified**.
- I found no source giving an explicit **coarsening exponent** (for example, whether pile count decays as a power law in t) for BM/LF-type models. Martin et al. (2002) may contain one, but only the abstract was accessible.
- Theraulaz's fitted constants are listed without their Greek symbols, which were lost in PDF extraction. The mapping of each value to dropping versus picking terms follows the legend's order and should be checked against the PNAS figure 3 legend.

## Q2. Puck-gathering collective robotics (Beckers, Holland & Deneubourg 1994; Holland & Melhuish 1999; Melhuish, Holland & Hoddell 1998; Martinoli et al. 1999; Gauci et al. 2014; Vardy; Kazadi): what makes clusters grow at their edges versus merge

### Takeaway
Robot clusters grow and shrink only at their **perimeters**, so geometry decides the winner. Larger clusters are bigger targets and are **harder to steal from**, so they gain more and lose less. Kazadi's theory turns this into a checkable condition: if the ratio of removals to deposits, g(n), **decreases with cluster size n**, the system converges to one cluster. Arena walls act like an infinitely large cluster and capture piles, a "boundary effect" that is useful or harmful depending on intent. Endgames are slow when two clusters of **equal size** sit far apart. Very simple pushing robots, even a single one, can cluster objects by **circling the object cloud and pushing items inward**. Giving robots a shared target site (Vardy's cache consensus) speeds convergence significantly.

### Cited Findings
**Beckers, Holland & Deneubourg (1994)**
- Citation: "From local actions to global tasks: stigmergy and collective robotics," in Brooks & Maes (eds.), *Artificial Life IV*, MIT Press, pp. 181–189 [PR, conference] — [BibSonomy](https://bibsonomy.org/bibtex/379b0283f3b3fecdb02ee37a129114e3)
- The robots could not tell whether they were moving objects, had no memory, and sensed local object density only as above or below a **fixed threshold** (a gripper force microswitch). Mechanism: the threshold rule forms small clusters first. Random gains and losses then make some clusters larger than others. Larger clusters were less likely to lose objects and more likely to gain them, so **the end state was a single large cluster** [PR, 2ND] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- Hardware: a C-shaped scoop with a force sensor, pucks pushed rather than gripped, a microswitch limiting how many pucks are carried, 2 IR sensors, a square arena, and a **deformable boundary wall** so that pucks trapped at the edge could still be scooped up [PR] — [Song, Kim & Shell 2012, Table 1](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)
- Edge erosion: in Beckers' setup a robot could strike a cluster almost **tangentially** and remove one or two pucks without triggering the gripper. This glancing contact is how clusters lose items at their edges [PR] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- Beckers et al. ran until **100%** of pucks were in a single cluster. They found that robot-to-robot collisions rose steeply with team size, causing a large drop in performance beyond a small number of robots [PR, 2ND] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)

**Holland & Melhuish (1999) and Melhuish et al.**
- Citation: Holland & Melhuish, "Stigmergy, self-organization, and sorting in collective robotics," *Artificial Life* 5(2):173–202 (1999). U-bots clustered and sorted Frisbees using a rule set simpler than any proposed for ant sorting, with no spatial orientation and no memory [PR] — [full text](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- Basic clustering (Exp. 2) reached a cluster of **40 Frisbees after 8 h 25 min**. Because runs were slow, they used a **90% criterion** instead of Beckers' 100%. The authors note the natural end state is a *dynamic equilibrium* [PR] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- **Intermediate clusters became hard to steal from.** With a rigid boundary and their gripper, a straight-line approach rarely hit a cluster at a point that removed a Frisbee without triggering the gripper unless the cluster was very ragged. The cause was geometric [PR] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- **Boundary bifurcation** (Exp. 3; 10 robots, 44 Frisbees in a regular grid; p = probability of *keeping* a Frisbee on meeting the wall):
  - p = 0 and p = 0.5: everything ended at the periphery with no grouping, even after 11 h.
  - p = 0.8 and 0.85: one large cluster formed **at the wall**, with about 15 single Frisbees scattered around the edge.
  - p = 0.88: results split. The first trial grew a wall cluster to the 40-Frisbee criterion, stable to 11 h 20 min. Of 5 repeats, 3 made a single wall cluster (at 9 h 25 min, 10 h 35 min and 13 h 10 min) and 2 made central clusters (40 Frisbees at 6 h 0 min; 35 at 7 h 35 min, with 9 left at the edge).
  - p = 0.9 and 0.95: two main central clusters after 5 h and 2.5 h.
  - p = 1: a single central cluster after 6 h 35 min.
  - The authors describe a **parametric bifurcation from wall to central clustering at p ≈ 0.88** [PR] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- **Equal-size deadlock:** with a pull-back distance of 2.6 diameters, completion times over 5 trials were 7.58, 2.75, 25.3, 11.7 and 4.50 h. The very long runs coincided with **two clusters of the same size on opposite sides** of the arena, which stayed stable for many hours [PR] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- Team size: collisions were counted for 1–13 robots over 20-minute runs. The increase was low and roughly linear, so results with up to 10 robots could be read without modelling robot–robot interference [PR] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- Melhuish, Holland & Hoddell, "Collective sorting and segregation in robots with minimal sensing," *Proc. 5th Int. Conf. Simulation of Adaptive Behavior* (SAB'98), pp. 465–470. It used a Beckers-like mechanism (grippers plus a force-threshold microswitch, 4 IR sensors, an optical colour sensor) for **two-type segregation** [PR, 2ND] — [Gauci et al. 2014 reference list](https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/22948/download)
- Melhuish, Sendova-Franks, Scholes, Horsfield & Welsby, "Ant-inspired sorting by robots: the importance of initial clustering," *J R Soc Interface* 3(7):235–242 (2006), doi:10.1098/rsif.2005.0081. It compared an algorithm with **density-based pick-up and distance-based (pull-back) drop** against one with density-based pick-up and drop. The first sorted only from **already clustered** starts: from random starts, simulated robots failed in 500,000 steps and real robots failed in 2 h. The second worked from either start. Setup: 5 U-bots with CMUcams, 45 pucks in 3 colours; simulations used 6 robots [PR] — [PMC1578749](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1578749/)

**Kazadi's convergence condition, tested on physical robots**
- Kazadi, Abdul-Khaliq & Goodman, "On the convergence of puck clustering systems," *Robotics and Autonomous Systems* 38(2):93–117 (2002) [PR] — [DBLP](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/pid/47/4104.html). Follow-up: Kazadi, Chung, Lee & Cho, "On the dynamics of (puck) clustering systems," *RAS* 46 (2004); the title differs slightly between listings [PR; not read] — [Elsevier Pure](https://imsa.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/on-the-dynamics-of-puck-clustering-systems/)
- Kazadi defines a **cluster formation function** g(n) = (total removals from clusters of size n)/(total deposits onto clusters of size n). A **sufficient condition for convergence to a single cluster is that g(n) is monotonically decreasing**. Song, Kim & Shell (ANTS 2012, likely LNCS 7461, doi:10.1007/978-3-642-32650-9_32) measured g(n) on physical robots: 5 iRobot Creates, 20 boxes 35 cm square, an octagonal 4.5 m × 4.5 m arena, 3 trials of 90 min per condition, more than 40 h in total. Except for the all-digger condition, g < 1 and decreasing, consistent with guaranteed single-cluster formation given enough time [PR] — [Song, Kim & Shell 2012](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)
- **Walls as giant clusters:** with a basic Beckers/Holland-style controller, most box clusters formed **on the boundary**, because walls behave like a large cluster and buttress partial piles. A video of 36 iRobot Creates from Vaughan's lab showed the same thing. Song et al. countered with open-loop "twisting" and "digging" motions that pry boxes off walls by hitting their corners [PR] — [Song, Kim & Shell 2012](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)
- Lineage, as Song et al. summarize it: Beckers gave a geometric explanation of cluster emergence, Martinoli quantified that geometric notion, and Kazadi supplied formal conditions [PR] — [Song, Kim & Shell 2012](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)

**Martinoli, Ijspeert & Mondada (1999) and Agassounon, Martinoli et al.: seed aggregation**
- Citation: "Understanding collective aggregation mechanisms: from probabilistic modelling to experiments with real robots," *RAS* 29(1):51–63 (1999), doi:10.1016/S0921-8890(99)00038-X [PR] — [EPFL record](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/58489). **Gauci, Chen, Li, Dodd & Groß (2014): clustering with robots that do not compute**
- AAMAS 2014, pp. 421–428. Each e-puck has one line-of-sight sensor with three readings (nothing / object / robot) and no range, no memory and no arithmetic. The controller is just **6 wheel speeds, a fixed pair for each sensor state**, evolved with CMA-ES. Fitness is a time-weighted **second moment** of object positions, so it rewards both compactness and speed [PR] — [paper PDF](https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/22948/download); [project page](https://sheffield.ac.uk/naturalrobotics/supp/2014-003)
- **Emergent mechanism:** robots first reach the periphery of the object cloud. They then **move in a circle around it, pushing each object slightly inward at every contact**, and branch out to encircle stray objects. The behaviour held across many settings and **also works with a single robot**. Objects very far from the rest are sometimes missed [PR] — [paper PDF](https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/22948/download)
- Scale and noise: simulations used 50 robots with 20 objects; the scalability study used 50 objects with 10–50 robots, 100 runs per setting, 1,000 s each; the noise study used 20 robots and 50 objects. Physical trials used 5 robots and 20 objects (EPS cylinders 10 cm across, about 35 g), 10 trials of 10 min: on average **86.5% of objects ended in the largest cluster**, and one trial reached a single cluster at about 300 s. Clustering works even if robots cannot tell objects from robots, but performs significantly better when they can [PR] — [paper PDF](https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/22948/download)
- In Maris & te Boekhorst's Didabots (IROS 1996, pp. 1655–1660), objects had to be **smaller than the gap between the two front sensors**, and **no clustering occurred with a single robot** [PR, 2ND] — [Gauci et al. 2014](https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/22948/download). Their robots never sensed cubes: they pushed them until an obstacle was detected, and cubes pushed against the wall counted as "lost" [PR, 2ND] — [Song, Kim & Shell 2012](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)

**Vardy and collaborators: committing to a cache site, and pushing**
- Vardy, Vorobyev & Banzhaf, "Cache consensus: rapid object sorting by a robotic swarm," *Swarm Intelligence* (2014). Each robot keeps a **cache point** for each object type and returns picked-up objects to it. Robots converge on the best cache by comparing observed clusters with remembered cache sizes, with no explicit communication. The authors report a **significant improvement in the rate of convergence** over purely probabilistic clustering [PR; abstract only] — [GP-bib entry](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/Vardy_2014_SwarmIntl.html)
- The MUN BOTS lab also studies **pushing-based** sorting and planar construction, in which robots push object classes into designated areas and around obstacles. This includes "orbital construction" (Vardy 2018) and a 2022 ANTS paper on sorting in complex environments [PRAC/lab page] — [BOTS lab projects](https://bots.cs.mun.ca/projects/)

### Inferences
- **Edges decide growth.** In every robot system above, clusters change only at their perimeter. Deposits scale with the target's cross-section, while removals need glancing hits that become geometrically rare on large, smooth clusters. Kazadi's monotonically decreasing g(n) is the formal version. In a deterministic single-agent game, *we choose g(n)*: never take from piles at or near t, take freely from piles below some size s_min, and deposit preferentially on the largest pile that is still below t. This satisfies the spirit of the convergence condition while respecting the cap.
- **Walls (the region boundary) act like large piles**: they stop pushes and buttress piles. For our game, boundary-adjacent pile sites are natural "sinks" for strokes, raking toward the fence or curb. They also lengthen the walk from the interior, and corners may leave stranded points if k is small.
- **Equal far-apart piles are the endgame trap**, seen in Holland & Melhuish's 25.3 h run and Theraulaz's 50% "opposite sides" outcome. A heuristic should detect two or more non-full piles whose combined size reaches t but which are far apart, and either merge them early or route the remaining loose points so that one pile clearly dominates.
- **Circling the perimeter and pushing inward** (Gauci) is a single-agent-compatible, connectivity-friendly pattern. Removing the outermost points first shrinks the hull while leaving interior stepping stones in place.
- **Committing to target sites** (Vardy's caches, LF's memory) is the swarm-robotics evidence for planning pile sites before gathering.
- **Initial clustering matters** (Melhuish et al. 2006). Form local piles first, then balance or sort them. In our game, capacity-balancing to exactly t is easier once points are already in micro-piles.

### Gaps
- Beckers et al.'s own experimental numbers are **unverified**: puck count, number of robots (often recalled as 1–5), optimal team size (often recalled as about 3), and the puck count that trips the gripper (often recalled as 3). Holland & Melhuish confirm only the qualitative interference result.
- Martinoli et al. (1999) body text was unreadable (bitmap fonts). The often-quoted mechanism is that robots drop seeds only next to other seeds and modify clusters only at their tips, so cluster count never increases. That is **unverified here** and should be checked before relying on it.
- No paper I found reports a closed-form law for cluster count over time in robot puck clustering. Only figures (cluster count against time) were seen.
- Vardy et al. (2014) volume and pages (often cited as 8(1):61–87) were not confirmed.

## Q3. Single-agent gathering with a pushing tool (sweeping points, snowblower and snowplow robots) and how pile footprint scales with mass

### Takeaway
Computational geometry has studied our problem in an idealized form: gathering points with a sweep-line tool, where swept points merge and cost is total sweep length. A simple **two-sweep, "line then point" strategy is within a factor of 4/π ≈ 1.27 of optimal**. The single-agent pushing problem with a **capacity limit on piles** (the snowblower problem) is **NP-complete** but admits constant-factor approximations, and those constants worsen as the throw or push directions become more restricted. Snow-removal robot patents converge on grid coverage plus pile-capacity-aware push patterns. Physically, a conical pile at angle of repose θ has volume V = (π/3) r³ tan θ, so **footprint radius ∝ V^{1/3}** and footprint area ∝ V^{2/3}.

### Cited Findings
- **Sweeping points (orthogonal model).** Points are pushed by a moving line; points swept over one another merge; cost is the total sweep length, with repositioning free. Dumitrescu & Jiang give a **4/π ≈ 1.27 approximation using at most 2 sweeps (4 if the target point is fixed), computable in O(n log n)**. Citation: A. Dumitrescu & M. Jiang, "Sweeping points," *Algorithmica*, doi:10.1007/s00453-009-9364-6; preliminary version in APPROX 2008, LNCS 5171, pp. 63–76 [PR, 2ND via the authors' follow-up] — [Dumitrescu & Jiang, "Sweeping an oval to a vanishing point," arXiv:1101.4667](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.4667)
- **Slanted sweeps / "crumb cleanup."** Bousany, Karker, O'Rourke & Sparaco (CCCG 2010) let the line translate along any vector. They conjectured that two sweeps along the sides of a minimum-perimeter enclosing parallelogram are always optimal. Dumitrescu & Jiang **disproved** this: some convex regions cannot be swept optimally with two sweeps, and possibly no finite sequence is optimal. Two-sweep algorithms based on the minimum-perimeter enclosing rectangle or parallelogram are still **4/π-approximations** [PREPRINT] — [arXiv:1101.4667](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.4667)
- **The snowblower problem.** Arkin, Bender, Mitchell & Polishchuk: find a short tour that clears a driveway or sidewalk, where the machine moves snow into nearby regions and **cannot clear a pile once it is too high**. The problem is **NP-complete**. Polynomial-time **constant-factor approximations** exist for three throw models (any direction; any direction except backwards; only to the right), and the constants grow as throwing becomes more restricted. The authors note applications to robotic vacuuming or mowing with a bounded-capacity bin and to rearranging cartons on a warehouse floor [PREPRINT; submitted to WAFR 2006] — [arXiv:cs/0603026](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0603026)
- **Snow-removal robot patents** describe three approaches. One discretizes the area into unit cells and plans passes, then re-plows leftover cells while limiting snow-bank height. One chooses among push patterns (parallel to the road, perpendicular from the middle, or S-shaped) according to road width and **pile capacity**, optionally with **fixed snow-piling points**. One uses potential-field grids [PATENT, SNIPPET] — [US 11993284](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11993284); [US 10920386](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10920386); [US 12650699](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12650699)
- **Blade capacity in practice:** for straight-blade plowing, a vendor guide advises engaging the full blade width for snow up to about 2 in, three-quarters for about 4 in and half for 6 in or more (that is, push only what the equipment can handle). It also says pushing forward is more effective than backdragging over long distances, and backdrags should be limited to about 2–3 truck lengths [PRAC] — [BOSS straight-blade guide](https://info.bossplow.com/blog/blog/bid/108097/how-to-guide-plowing-with-your-boss-snowplow-straight-blade). A V-blade in "scoop" position piles snow in its centre and is said to carry up to 3× the volume of the straight position [PRAC, SNIPPET] — [BOSS manual](https://content.bossplow.com/content/servicecenter/documents/msc04222.pdf)
- **Pile footprint physics:** a conical stockpile has V = (1/3)πr²h with repose angle θ = arctan(h/r) [PRAC/engineering calculator] — [Firgelli angle-of-repose calculator](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/engineering-calculators/angle-of-repose-calculator); the equivalent imperial rule is V = 0.2618·D²·h (π/12) [PRAC] — [bulk-online stockpile estimates](https://www.bulk-online.com/en/node/70570). Substituting h = r·tanθ gives V = (π/3)·r³·tanθ, so r = (3V/(π·tanθ))^{1/3} (algebra; no extra source needed)
- **Robotic analogue of footprint feedback:** larger clusters are bigger targets and harder to erode (Beckers; Holland & Melhuish; see Q2) [PR] — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)

### Inferences
- **"Line then point" is the theoretically best-supported gathering primitive** for sweep tools. With an unbounded sweep line, collapsing the cloud to a line and then to a point costs at most about 27% more than optimal. In our game the tool is a **finite capsule** (half-width l) and the target is **n piles of exactly t**, so the analogue is: collapse to **windrows** (rows of micro-piles), then collapse each row into piles of t (a 1-D partition problem). The 1.27 bound does not transfer directly; this is a motivation, not a guarantee.
- **The capacity constraint changes the complexity class.** The snowblower result says that adding a pile-capacity limit to coverage makes the single-agent problem NP-complete, and that restricting directions inflates the approximation constants. Expect our exact-t game, with its reach limit k and no-teleport rule, to be at least as hard. That justifies heuristics plus local search, and suggests **benchmarking against lower bounds** such as the sum of distances to the nearest pile site or a capacitated assignment relaxation.
- **Choosing a footprint model (optional game rule):** a 3-D cone gives r(c) = r₀·c^{1/3}. A flat 2-D mat of fixed density gives r(c) = r₀·c^{1/2}. For t = 50 the radius grows 3.7× (cone) versus 7.1× (mat). Under either, big piles are **cheaper merge targets** (shorter edge-to-edge strokes) and **catch more capsule sweeps**. That is positive feedback in the Kazadi sense (deposit rate rising with size), which favours few large piles but **raises overfill risk near capacity**. If the rule is adopted, the cone (c^{1/3}) is the physically grounded default, and the strength of the feedback should be a tunable parameter.
- **Pass sizing suggests capacity-aware stroke selection.** A plow operator engages part of the blade in deep snow, and a balanced design matches windrow volume to machine capacity (Q4). The game analogue is choosing stroke geometry (endpoints, angle) so the capsule captures **at most t − current** loose points, for example by routing the segment through empty space when the target pile is nearly full.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed robot study of a **single agent pushing many objects into multiple capacity-limited piles** (as opposed to swarms or coverage). Literature on push planning and rearrangement planning exists but was not surveyed here.
- No measured **angle of repose for leaf litter or snow** was found, so the cone constant (tan θ) for leaves is unknown.
- Snowblower approximation constants and the Dumitrescu–Jiang lower bounds were not extracted (abstract and follow-up only).

## Q4. Real-world leaf collection, snow plowing and raking practice: what operations teach about efficient gathering

### Takeaway
Field operations consistently use **two-stage gathering**: first windrow the material into a narrow row along a line the collector will pass (the curb, the road shoulder, a hay windrow), then **collect along that line** with a high-capacity machine on a planned route, without backtracking. Practitioners **size windrows to match collector capacity** (hay mergers) and **pass material from one pass to the next** (echelon plowing). Routing at scale is formalized as **arc routing** (snow plowing, street sweeping) or node routing over **deposit points** (bagged leaves). Advice on manual raking (tarps, small piles, working with the wind) is practitioner lore. I found **no peer-reviewed time-and-motion study** of manual raking.

### Cited Findings
**Municipal leaf collection (windrow, then vacuum)**
- Latrobe, PA: residents put leaves at the curb in a **windrow no wider than two feet** that may run the length of the property. Plastic bags are prohibited and only biodegradable bags are allowed. Collection uses a **leaf vacuum towed by a dump truck**. Crews work **ward by ward**, do not move to the next ward until every street in the current one is done, and **do not return to a street until the whole city has been collected**. No exact dates are published, and at peak season crews may cover only a couple of streets a day [PRAC/municipal] — [City of Latrobe](https://cityoflatrobe.com/services/public_works/leaf_collection.php)
- Other programs use rotating zones (Harrison Township, NJ: six zones in a weekly rotation) and a fixed number of passes per street per season (South Windsor, CT: two) [PRAC, SNIPPET] — [Harrison Twp article](https://thesunpapers.com/2019/10/14/as-leaf-pick-up-begins-harrison-township-reminds-residents-how-to-properly-place-leaves-on-curb/); [South Windsor](https://www.southwindsor-ct.gov/node/26273)
- **Optimizing leaf sweeping and collection (Trenque Lauquen, Argentina):** integer linear programs assign sweepers to city blocks, choose **leaf-bag deposit points**, and route bag-collection trucks, with truck routes modelled as an **asymmetric TSP**. Before this, assignments were improvised and blocks went unswept. Projections: about **half as many deposit points** and **10–15% less truck travel**. Citation: Delle Donne, Di Tomaso & Durán, *Waste Management & Research* (online 2020), doi:10.1177/0734242X20922597 [PR; projected, not measured] — [U. Chile repository](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/handle/2250/176108)

**Snow plowing practice and routing**
- **Tandem (echelon) plowing:** plows run diagonally staggered, each behind and to the right or left of the one ahead, so a group clears **all lanes in one sweep**. Colorado DOT calls it the safest and most efficient method; it leaves ridges of snow between lanes as the windrow is handed from plow to plow [PRAC/state DOT] — [CDOT tandem snowplow fact sheet](https://codot.gov/travel/winter-driving/assets/campaign-materials/winter-wise-tandem-snowplow-law-fact-sheet.pdf). Highway plows push snow, slush and debris **to the right** of the truck [PRAC, SNIPPET] — [CDOT news release](https://www.codot.gov/news/2016-news-releases/12-2016/cdot-reminds-motorists-do-not-crowd-snow-plow-trucks)

Finish with a straight blade pushing ahead to clean up spillage, and finish the job once begun, because leftover windrows can freeze overnight [PRAC/vendor] — [BOSS straight-blade guide](https://info.bossplow.com/blog/blog/bid/108097/how-to-guide-plowing-with-your-boss-snowplow-straight-blade)

### Inferences
- **Windrow-then-collect is universal** in field practice (curb leaf windrows plus vacuum, hay windrows plus baler, snow windrows plus plow passes). It turns a 2-D gathering problem into a **1-D collection problem along a route**. In our game, rows of micro-piles spaced at most k apart double as **stepping-stone highways** that prevent stranding.
- **Size the windrow to the collector** (hay mergers). Merge rows until a row segment holds about t points, so that one sweep along the row, or a short run of merges, completes exactly one pile.
- **Never return to a finished street** (Latrobe's ward rule) and the **echelon handoff** (each pass pushes the previous windrow further) both point to a **monotone sweep front**. Process the region in a fixed order so that finished piles lie behind the front and are never re-swept, which also limits accidental capsule capture by full piles' surroundings.
- **Deposit-point selection plus routing** (Trenque Lauquen) is the operations-research form of choosing pile sites and then the gathering tour, and halving the number of deposit points was the projected major saving. Choose pile sites well and the routing follows.
- **"Small piles then tarp" versus "tarp from the start"** in the raking guides is the micro-piles-then-merge versus snowball trade-off. Small piles reduce stroke length per point; a tarp, which is a mobile pile, avoids double handling.
- Ergonomic advice (forward and back strokes, no twisting) suggests that if the game's cost model wants realism, it could add a **turning or direction-change penalty** and favour long straight drags toward the pile.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed time-and-motion study of manual leaf raking** was found. Pine-straw harvesting and landscaping productivity studies may exist but were not searched.
- **No university extension guidance on raking technique** (stroke patterns, pile spacing) was found. Extension sources focus on leaf reuse. The tarp and wind advice is consumer-guide lore.
- I found no peer-reviewed study on **routing municipal leaf vacuum trucks** (as opposed to bag collection or snow). Bodin & Kursh's street-sweeper routing paper (*Operations Research*, usually cited as 1978, 26(4):525–537; one search summary said 1987) was not checked.
- Some municipal details (Harrison Twp zones, South Windsor passes) and the UW Extension merger text are search-snippet level only.

## Q5. Candidate heuristics for the leaf-raking game, with when each should do well or badly

### Takeaway
The evidence points to a few transferable principles. (a) **Plan pile sites first, globally** (LALI spacing, cache consensus, deposit-point optimization), because local rules get the pile count wrong. (b) **Grow the largest pile that is below capacity, and never take from near-full piles** (Kazadi's decreasing g(n), with a cap at t). (c) **Use two stages, windrows or micro-piles then merge** (sweeping-points 2-sweep bound, curb and hay windrows, AntClass). (d) **Work from the outside in, or behind a monotone front**, so remaining objects stay connected (Gauci's circling; never re-visit a finished street). (e) **Avoid the equal-and-far-apart endgame** (Theraulaz; Holland & Melhuish). Eight concrete heuristics follow, each with expected strengths and weaknesses.

### Cited Findings
(The evidence behind each heuristic; full citations are in Q1–Q4.)
- Two sweeps (line, then point) are within 4/π of optimal for line-sweeping points to a single target — [arXiv:1101.4667](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.4667)
- Pile count from local rules scales with density × area, nearby piles compete within a critical distance, and there is a critical density for nucleation — [Theraulaz et al. 2002](https://static.ias.edu/pitp/archive/2012files/44.pdf); [Sakiyama 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7012894/)
- Convergence to one cluster if removals/deposits g(n) decreases with size — [Song, Kim & Shell 2012](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/papers/ants2012clustering.pdf)
- Large clusters are hard to erode; equal far-apart clusters persist for hours; walls capture clusters — [Holland & Melhuish 1999](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs689/papers/holland99stigmergy.pdf)
- Circling the periphery and pushing inward clusters objects, even with one robot — [Gauci et al. 2014](https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/22948/download)
- Memory or commitment to target sites reduces duplicate clusters and speeds convergence — [Ramos & Merelo on LF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0412075); [Vardy et al. 2014](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/Vardy_2014_SwarmIntl.html)
- Two-pass heaps (many tight clusters, then heaps treated as atoms and merged) — [Schockaert et al. 2004](https://faculty.washington.edu/mdecock/papers/sschockaert2004.pdf)
- Initial clustering is a precondition for some sorting rules — [Melhuish et al. 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1578749/)
- Curb windrows of at most 2 ft, ward-by-ward collection with no return — [Latrobe](https://cityoflatrobe.com/services/public_works/leaf_collection.php); echelon plowing hands windrows between passes — [CDOT](https://codot.gov/travel/winter-driving/assets/campaign-materials/winter-wise-tandem-snowplow-law-fact-sheet.pdf); merging windrows matches capacity — [UW Extension, SNIPPET](https://fyi.extension.wisc.edu/forage/rakers-and-mergers)
- Pass sizing (partial blade in deep snow) — [BOSS](https://info.bossplow.com/blog/blog/bid/108097/how-to-guide-plowing-with-your-boss-snowplow-straight-blade); capacity-limited pushing is NP-complete — [Arkin et al. 2006](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0603026)
- Optimizing deposit points (about half as many) and truck routes (−10–15%, projected) — [Delle Donne et al.](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/handle/2250/176108)

### Inferences
*Assumptions (to be checked against the simulator spec):* a pile stays where it is founded, so object locations only ever **shrink to a subset of the original point positions**; strokes move objects only *into* existing piles; cost is stroke length plus walking, possibly weighted by dragged mass. Under these assumptions the k-disk graph over occupied sites only **loses vertices** over time. Stranding prevention is therefore about the **order in which vertices are removed**, and **pile sites are permanent stepping stones**. This structural point is my inference, not from the literature.

**H0 (safety wrapper for every heuristic): connectivity guard.** Before each stroke, simulate its effect, including capsule captures. Reject it if any remaining loose point or non-full pile would become unreachable from the agent's next position in the k-graph over remaining objects. Use articulation-point checks; recomputing is cheap at game sizes. Prefer orders that remove "leaf" vertices first, i.e. post-order along a spanning tree rooted at pile sites, which is the "rake from the far fence back toward the pile" rule. *Grounding:* no-return ward sweeps and echelon fronts (Q4) and Gauci's outside-in pushing (Q2). *Cost:* it may force detours; measure that overhead.

**H1: Windrow-then-pile (two-stage, "line then point").**
- Pick a sweep direction, for example the long side of the minimum-perimeter enclosing rectangle (Dumitrescu–Jiang) or the principal axis of the cloud.
- Lay rows of row-pile sites perpendicular to the sweep, with rows about 2l apart (so each capsule pass covers a strip) and sites within a row at most k apart (a connected highway).
- Stage 1: drag points perpendicular into the nearest row site, with the capsule catching neighbours.
- Stage 2: along each row, partition the consecutive micro-piles into contiguous groups of exactly t (1-D DP; allow small cross-row transfers when a row's total is not a multiple of t) and merge each group.
- *Good when:* fairly uniform or elongated clouds; large l (capsules align with rows); D_k ≳ row spacing; mass-independent cost.
- *Bad when:* strong clustering (rows cut through clusters, leaving partial piles), k smaller than inter-cluster gaps, or very dense rows that overfill. Each point is moved twice.
- *Variant H1b, echelon front:* push each strip's material into the next strip ahead, dropping finished t-piles behind the front (snowplow handoff).

**H2: LALI / density-peak pile siting with capacitated assignment.**
- Estimate local density with a kernel of bandwidth about k or l (the LF-style f).
- Choose n sites at density peaks, separated by an inhibition radius of about √(A/(πn)) (Theraulaz's critical distance). Refine with capacitated k-medoids or Lloyd iterations so each site's catchment holds exactly t points (a min-cost-flow assignment).
- Gather each catchment toward its site, ordered by H0.
- *Good when:* natural clusters hold roughly multiples of t points; it minimizes total drag distance and mirrors deposit-point optimization.
- *Bad when:* cluster sizes are far from multiples of t (forcing long boundary-crossing strokes), sparse regions fall below the "critical density" (sites stranded or far apart), or the assignment ignores capsule side-captures (needs a capture-aware cost).

**H3: Grow-the-largest, capped ("preferential attachment").**
- Repeatedly take the nearest reachable loose point (or a pile below s_min) and drag it into the **largest non-full pile within reach**.
- **Never take from piles of size ≥ s_protect**; this gives the decreasing g(n) Kazadi requires.
- Close a pile as soon as it reaches t; a closed pile becomes an inert stepping stone.
- Found a new pile only if no non-full pile is in reach.
- Merge two non-full piles within k when their sum is ≤ t (prefer unequal pairs).
- *Expected pile-count curve:* rise, peak, fall (as in the ants). Log it as a diagnostic.
- *Good when:* D_k ≈ t; highly local, robust, cheap to compute.
- *Bad when:* the end state leaves straggler partial piles far apart (the equal-far-apart trap and the ~15 peripheral singletons seen in Holland & Melhuish), or capsule overfill of near-full piles occurs. It can also eat bridge points unless H0 guards it.

**H4: Snowball / tarp (mobile pile).**
- Keep one active pile and repeatedly **drag the whole active pile** to the next point along a planned tour (a TSP or boustrophedon path over the k-graph), so the capsule sweeps up loose points along each segment.
- When it reaches t (or a stroke would overfill), stop, close it and start a new snowball nearby.
- *Good when:* cost is **not** mass-weighted, l is large relative to point spacing (each drag "plows" many points), and the stroke count matters more than distance.
- *Bad when:* cost scales with dragged mass (as with heavy snow or a loaded tarp). Capacity overshoot mid-stroke must be prevented by shortening or re-aiming strokes (BOSS pass sizing), and the snowblower result implies no simple optimal tour.

**H5: Perimeter-inward (hull peeling, Gauci circling).**
- Walk the outer boundary of the remaining loose set, on points or piles.
- At each step, drag the outermost reachable points **inward** into the nearest interior non-full pile, choosing an inward angle that maximizes capsule capture without overfilling.
- Repeat, shrinking the hull layer by layer. Interior piles fill toward t and close.
- *Good when:* convex-ish single blobs; inherently preserves interior connectivity (low stranding risk); works with one agent, as in Gauci.
- *Bad when:* clouds are multi-cluster or concave with gaps larger than k (the perimeter walk breaks), the hull is large and the walking overhead high, or inward sweeps spill points into the wrong pile, upsetting exact t.

**H6: Micro-piles then merge (AntClass two-pass; "small piles then tarp").**
- Pass 1: greedily form many micro-piles of target size m using only short strokes inside k-neighbourhoods. Choose m so that t is an easy sum, for example m = t/2, t/3 or a mix of sizes that pack well.
- Pass 2: solve a **capacitated merging problem** over micro-piles (bin packing with distance costs, or a small VRP-like search) and execute the merges along k-chains, merging leaves of the micro-pile spanning tree first.
- *Good when:* the **exact-t constraint** is tight (small "coins" make exact change easy), data are clustered, and connectivity is fragile (micro-piles act as stepping stones).
- *Bad when:* double handling of every point is costly (in distance, or more so in mass-weighted cost) or micro-pile sizes are badly chosen; LF-style over-fragmentation then means long merge chains.

**H7: Boundary-anchored piles (wall / curb / fence effect).**
- Put pile sites along the region boundary at spacing about t/(boundary density of points), and drag points **outward** toward the nearest boundary site (rake to the curb or fence).
- Combine with H1 by making the rows perpendicular to the nearest edge.
- *Good when:* elongated or strip-like regions, points concentrated near edges, or large l (pushing toward a wall "plows" the whole strip). Full piles near the edge are out of the way of later strokes.
- *Bad when:* square regions with central mass (long strokes), corners (cramped approaches, overfill from converging sweeps), and when interior connectivity depends on removed points.

**H8: Anti-stalemate endgame rule ("break symmetry early").**
- An add-on to H2, H3 and H6. Monitor all non-full piles. If two or more non-full piles together hold about t points but are farther apart than about 2k, act now while bridges still exist: either merge them through a chain of piles, or redirect the remaining nearby loose points to make one clearly dominant. Equal, distant, half-full piles persisted for hours in robots and were the modal ant outcome.
- *Good:* cuts worst-case endgame cost. *Bad:* may trigger early merges that later turn out unnecessary.

**Suggested test design (inference):**
- Generate instances with mild clustering (for example Thomas or Matérn cluster processes of varying cluster radius and parent intensity).
- Sweep the dimensionless groups D_k = λπk² / t and capture per stroke 2lλ·(mean stroke length) / t, plus t itself (small t favours H3 and H5; large t favours H1, H4 and H6).
- Use two cost models: distance only, and distance × dragged mass.
- Report total cost, **stranding / infeasibility rate**, number of strokes, overfill near-misses, the **pile-count trajectory** (expect rise, peak, fall for local heuristics and flat for planned ones), and deadheading (walking without dragging) as a fraction of cost, a standard arc-routing efficiency measure.
- Baselines: a lower bound from capacitated assignment of points to optimal sites, and the "found, then always drag to nearest open pile" greedy rule.

### Gaps
- None of the cited works addresses an **exact pile-size requirement** or a **reach (no-teleport) constraint** for a single agent. The mapping above is inference and needs testing in the simulator.
- The effect of the capsule (side-capture) on the snowblower and sweeping-points complexity results is unknown; those models capture everything under an unbounded line or move material by fixed throw rules.
- The structural claim that object locations only shrink and pile sites are permanent stepping stones depends on simulator rules I have not seen (for example, whether the destination of a stroke must also be within k, and where the agent stands after a stroke).
