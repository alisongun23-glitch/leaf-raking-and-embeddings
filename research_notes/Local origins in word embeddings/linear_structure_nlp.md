# Linear and directional structure in word/token embeddings: what the NLP/ML literature actually shows

Scope: whether semantic relations are encoded as directions/offsets in embedding space, what centering (global and per-cluster) does, how "concept directions" are found and validated, and whether anything in the literature matches "center a cluster of related words on its own mean, run PCA, read the top eigenvector as the relation". Each technique gets a one-sentence plain-English gloss (marked *Gloss*). Numbers come from the papers' own tables unless flagged.

Glossary for non-NLP readers:
- *Embedding*: a list of ~300 (word2vec/GloVe) to ~4,000 (LLaMA-2) numbers per word/token, learned so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors.
- *Static* embeddings (word2vec, GloVe, fastText): one vector per word. *Contextual* embeddings (ELMo, BERT, GPT-2, LLaMA): one vector per token occurrence, depending on the sentence.
- *Offset*: the vector difference between two words, e.g. queen − king.
- *Cosine similarity*: the cosine of the angle between two vectors (1 = same direction, 0 = orthogonal).
- *PCA / principal component*: the direction along which a set of points varies most; the second component is the next-most-varying direction orthogonal to the first, etc. "Eigenvector" in the learner's proposal = principal component of the cluster's covariance.
- *Isotropy*: the vectors point roughly uniformly in all directions (no dominant direction); *anisotropy*: they crowd into a narrow cone.

## Key question 1: Word analogies and offset vectors — what the analogy test measures, and which relations actually show consistent offsets

### Takeaway
Vector offsets do capture some relations (notably inflectional morphology, country–capital, gender), but the standard analogy test is confounded with nearest-neighbour structure and excludes the query words; on balanced test sets (BATS) the offset method solves under 30% of questions, with derivational morphology and lexicographic relations (hypernymy, meronymy, synonymy) near 0–15%. Theory (Ethayarajh et al. 2019) says offsets are parallel only when a specific co-occurrence statistic (csPMI) is constant across pairs, which holds for few relations; kinship ("family") has one of the *highest* csPMI variances in the Google set, i.e. its offsets are comparatively non-uniform even though 3CosAdd accuracy is 0.836.

### Cited Findings

**The original claim (2013)**
- *Gloss: Mikolov et al. noticed that subtracting and adding word vectors ("King − Man + Woman") lands near "Queen", and proposed solving a:b::c:? by nearest neighbour to b − a + c.* The NAACL 2013 paper reports that RNN word vectors "correctly answer almost 40% of" a syntactic analogy test set (RNN-1600: 39.6% overall; adjectives 23.9%, nouns 29.2%, verbs 62.2%), and that semantic regularities were shown on SemEval-2012 Task 2. — [Mikolov, Yih & Zweig 2013, NAACL](https://aclanthology.org/N13-1090/)
- Arora et al. (2016) name the phenomenon "relations=lines": for a relation R there is a direction μ_R such that for any pair (a,b) in the relation, v_a − v_b "is like μ_R plus some noise vector", derived under a random-walk generative model whose key assumption is that word vectors are isotropic (uniformly spread) in the bulk. — [Arora, Li, Liang, Ma, Risteski 2016, TACL (arXiv 1502.03520)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.03520)

**3CosAdd vs 3CosMul and the PMI explanation (Levy & Goldberg 2014)**
- *Gloss: 3CosAdd scores candidates by cos(b*,b) − cos(b*,a) + cos(b*,a*); 3CosMul multiplies/divides the same cosines instead, which stops one large similarity from swamping the others.* The multiplicative objective is argmax cos(b*,b)·cos(b*,a*)/(cos(b*,a)+ε). Results: on word2vec embeddings 3CosAdd gets 53.98% (MSR) / 62.70% (Google); 3CosMul 59.09% / 66.72%. On *explicit* sparse PPMI count vectors (no neural training at all) 3CosMul reaches 56.83% / 68.24% — i.e. the "magic" of vector arithmetic is largely a property of co-occurrence statistics, not of neural embeddings. — [Levy & Goldberg 2014, CoNLL "Linguistic Regularities in Sparse and Explicit Word Representations"](https://aclanthology.org/W14-1618/)

**The analogy test is confounded with neighbourhood structure**
- Linzen (2016): with no exclusion of query words ("Vanilla"), the nearest neighbour of a* − a + b was b itself in 93% of cases and a* in 5%; a baseline that ignores the offset entirely and just returns the nearest neighbour of b ("Only-b") reaches 0.70 on plurals; "Ignore-a" beat Only-b in 10 of 14 categories; the offset method "conflates offset consistency with largely irrelevant neighborhood structure". Reversing the direction of the analogy dropped accuracy by a mean of −0.11 (US cities 0.69 → 0.17; common capitals 0.90 → 0.53). — [Linzen 2016, "Issues in evaluating semantic spaces using word analogies"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07736)
- Rogers, Drozd & Matsuoka (2017): on BATS, when the source vectors a, a′, b are *not* excluded, b is the most likely prediction in all four relation types, a′ is predicted in under 30% of derivational/encyclopedic cases, and the true answer b′ "is as unlikely to be predicted as a, or any other vector" — "the addition of the offset between a and a′ typically has a very small effect on the b vector". Under this "honest" 3CosAdd, top-1 accuracy was 0.08% (derivational), 0.59% (inflectional), 0.26% (encyclopedic). For nearly 40% of successfully solved questions b′ was within 10 nearest neighbours of b, and over 40% of low-accuracy questions had b′ more than 90 neighbours away. The best GloVe model scores under 30% on BATS overall. — [Rogers, Drozd & Matsuoka 2017, *SEM](https://aclanthology.org/S17-1017/)
- Nissim, van Noord & van der Goot (2020): in the unconstrained setting 3CosAdd/3CosMul performance drops sharply because "the second term is returned as answer (man is to king as woman is to king, thus D == B)"; with the original code "it is impossible to obtain man is to doctor as woman is to doctor", so famous bias analogies (doctor→nurse) are partly artefacts of the exclusion rule. — [Nissim et al. 2020, Computational Linguistics (arXiv 1905.09866)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09866)

**Which relations show reliable offsets (BATS, 40 relations × 4 types)**
- Gladkova, Drozd & Matsuoka (2016) built BATS (balanced across inflectional morphology, derivational morphology, lexicographic semantics, encyclopedic semantics). GloVe gets 80.4% on the Google set but average accuracy 0.285 on BATS (SVD: 0.221). "Only 3 categories out of ten [derivational] yield even 20% accuracy"; lexicographic relations are "so difficult to detect with the vector offset method" despite BATS accepting several correct answers. Per-category accuracies for a 1000-d SVD model (window 2–8): inflectional I01 58–71%, I06 53–71%; hypernyms L01 (cat:feline) 6–11%; L05 0–2%; L10 (binary antonyms, the "easiest" lexicographic category) 24–33%; derivational D01 0% at every window; encyclopedic E01 69–79% but E08 0%. — [Gladkova, Drozd & Matsuoka 2016, NAACL SRW](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002/)
- Drozd, Gladkova & Matsuoka (2016): on BATS the best 3CosAdd average is 28.1% (GloVe); 3CosAvg 34.4%; a supervised set-based method LRCos reaches 47.7% (Skip-Gram), with up to 34% gain on derivational relations — showing the information is partly present in the space "just not recoverable with 3CosAdd". In the singular–plural noun category "the correct answers are obtained with 70% accuracy as simply the nearest" neighbour of b. — [Drozd, Gladkova & Matsuoka 2016, COLING](https://aclanthology.org/C16-1332/)

**Why/when offsets are parallel: the csPMI theorem (Ethayarajh, Duvenaud & Hirst 2019)**
- *Gloss: co-occurrence shifted PMI, csPMI(x,y) = PMI(x,y) + log p(x,y), measures how much more often two words co-occur than chance, boosted by how common the pair is; the theorem says the parallelogram a:b::x:y holds exactly in a noise-free SGNS/GloVe space iff csPMI(a,b) = csPMI(x,y) and csPMI(a,x) = csPMI(b,y) (plus a coplanarity condition).* Corollaries: relations are ratios of co-occurrence probabilities; adding two vectors automatically down-weights the more frequent word ("the sum has more in common with the rarer word"); squared Euclidean distance is a linear function of −csPMI (empirical r = 0.50–0.51). — [Ethayarajh, Duvenaud & Hirst 2019, ACL](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04882)
- Empirically (their Table 1, Google analogy set): accuracy correlates with csPMI variance at Pearson r = −0.70 (p < 0.01). Low-variance/high-accuracy: capital-common-countries (var 0.345, acc 0.954), capital-world (0.496, 0.932), gram8-plural (0.590, 0.877). High-variance: currency (4.008, acc 0.092, median pair frequency 19), gram2-opposite (3.003, 0.554), city-in-state (2.979, 0.744), **family (2.897, acc 0.836)**, gram5-present-participle (2.969, 0.663). The table has no "gender" row; king/queen appears only as a text example. Unsolvable cases are attributed to polysemy and low frequency, not to a missing direction. — [same source, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04882)
- Allen & Hospedales (2019) give an alternative derivation: analogies follow from a probabilistic notion of *paraphrasing* ("king paraphrases man and royal"), proving "existence of linear relationships between W2V-type embeddings that underlie the analogical phenomenon, identifying explicit error terms"; they note competing explanations "each introduce assumptions that do not hold in practice". — [Allen & Hospedales 2019, ICML](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09813)

### Inferences
- The honest reading of the 2013 result is "offsets for a handful of relation types are roughly constant *and* the target lives near b"; the literature since 2016 treats the Google-set numbers (60–80%) as inflated and BATS (~28% for 3CosAdd) as the realistic figure.
- Relations that work best are those where the two words have near-identical distributional contexts except for one feature (plural vs singular, country vs capital, he vs she). Relations that change part of speech, or lexicographic relations (hypernymy, meronymy), are the weakest. Kinship sits in the middle: 0.836 accuracy on 3CosAdd but among the highest csPMI variances, so "the" family offset is noisier than the gender or plural offset.
- The csPMI result is the key theoretical constraint for the learner's proposal: a single direction encodes a relation only to the extent that the co-occurrence statistic is constant across the pairs; it is a property of *pairs*, not of a cluster's spread.

### Gaps
- I did not find a paper that reports the "fraction of relation types with reliable offsets" as a single number; BATS per-category tables are the closest (40 categories; roughly the 10 inflectional plus a few encyclopedic categories exceed 50%, most lexicographic/derivational under 15%, from the SVD table above). The GloVe/word2vec per-category table in Gladkova et al. is a figure I could not read numerically.
- Levy & Goldberg's 2014 NeurIPS paper ("Neural word embedding as implicit matrix factorization", the SGNS ≈ shifted-PMI factorization result) was not fetched; the csPMI theorem above builds on it.

## Key question 2: Anisotropy and centering — the common mean vector, dominant directions, global vs cluster-level isotropy

### Takeaway
Static and contextual embeddings share a large common mean vector and a few dominant directions that mostly encode frequency and syntax rather than meaning; subtracting the mean and removing the top ~d/100 principal components (globally) improves similarity tasks, and for contextual models doing the same *per k-means cluster* (zero-mean each cluster, remove its own top PCs) works better still — but the within-cluster dominant directions those papers find encode tense, punctuation/stop-word structure, and frequency, and the method's purpose is to *discard* them.

### Cited Findings

**Static embeddings: All-but-the-Top (Mu, Bhat & Viswanath, ICLR 2018)**
- *Gloss: subtract the average vector from every word, then delete the few directions along which the words vary most, because those directions are shared junk rather than meaning.* The common mean vector μ has norm "approximately 1/6 to 1/2" of the average word-vector norm (Table 1: word2vec 0.69/2.04 ≈ 0.34; GloVe 3.15/8.30 ≈ 0.38; CBOW ≈ 0.25; Skip-Gram ≈ 0.54). The spectrum decays near-exponentially at first, so most "energy" is in "a very low dimensional subspace (say, 8 dimensions out of 300)". Algorithm: subtract μ, PCA, remove projections on the top D components with D ≈ d/100 (D = 3 for word2vec, 2 for GloVe). — [Mu, Bhat & Viswanath 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01417)
- Isotropy measure I(W) = min_c Z(c) / max_c Z(c) over unit c (Z is Arora's partition function; 1 = perfectly isotropic): word2vec 0.7 → 0.95; GloVe 0.065 → 0.6 after processing. Word similarity (Spearman×100): e.g. GloVe SimLex 40.83 → 44.97, RW 46.41 → 52.04, but GloVe RG65 76.96 → 74.36 (a drop, despite the paper's claim of consistent improvement); average gain reported as 2.3%. Analogy barely moves (word2vec all 72.93 → 73.44; GloVe 76.89 → 77.15), which the paper explains by noting the mean and dominant components cancel out in the offset subtraction. Concept categorization (cluster purity): word2vec Battig 71.97 → 81.71, ESSLLI 75.00 → 84.09; GloVe ESSLLI and Battig unchanged. — [same source, Tables 3–5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01417)

**Contextual embeddings are anisotropic (Ethayarajh, EMNLP 2019)**
- *Gloss: in BERT/GPT-2, even unrelated tokens' vectors point in nearly the same direction, so raw cosine similarity is inflated and must be corrected by subtracting a random-word baseline.* GPT-2's average cosine between random words is "roughly 0.6" in layers 2–8 and rises so that in the last layer "any two words have on average an almost perfect cosine similarity". Self-similarity of the same word across contexts falls with depth. Maximum explainable variance (share of a word's contextual-representation variance captured by its first principal component, i.e. a PCA "static" embedding of one word): after anisotropy adjustment "in no layer of ELMo, BERT, or GPT-2 can more than 5% of the variance" be explained; unadjusted GPT-2 MEV is ~30% in layers 2–11 purely because of anisotropy. Every measure is corrected by subtracting a baseline computed on 1K random representations per layer. — [Ethayarajh 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.00512)

**Cluster-level centering: Rajaee & Pilehvar (ACL 2021) — the closest published match to "center a cluster on its own mean"**
- *Gloss: split the token vectors into k-means clusters, subtract each cluster's own mean, run PCA inside each cluster, and delete each cluster's top few directions.* Method: embeddings are clustered with k-means; "make each cluster zero-mean"; PCA is applied to each cluster and its top PCs are nulled out "separately". Tuned hyperparameters (STS-B dev): clusters 10 (GPT-2), 27 (BERT), 27 (RoBERTa); PCs removed per cluster 30 (GPT-2), 12 (BERT, RoBERTa); the global baseline (Mu & Viswanath) removes 30/15/25 PCs over the whole space. — [Rajaee & Pilehvar 2021, ACL (arXiv 2106.01183)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01183); ACL Anthology ID 2021.acl-short.73 ([listing](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2021.acl-short.73))
- Isotropy I(W) is astronomically low globally: layer-12 baselines GPT-2 5.0E-174, BERT 5.0E-05, RoBERTa 2.7E-06. Merely zero-meaning the whole space (k = 1) gives BERT 0.010, RoBERTa 0.015; zero-meaning each of k = 20 clusters (no PC removal) gives BERT 0.262 and RoBERTa 0.603 — "when the embedding space is viewed closely, the distribution of CWRs is notably more isotropic" — while GPT-2 stays near zero (2.49E-220 at k = 1), which "contradicts with the observation of Cai et al. (2021)"; the authors attribute the disagreement to Cai et al. measuring isotropy by cosine similarity rather than the partition-function ratio. Full method: GPT-2 2.6E-105 → 0.51 (global) → 0.70 (cluster-based); BERT 1.1E-04 → 0.58 → 0.73; RoBERTa 2.9E-06 → 0.88 → 0.90. — [same source, Tables 1, 5, 6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01183)
- Downstream (STS-B Spearman): BERT-base 47.4 → 65.5 (global) → 66.0 (cluster); RoBERTa-base 56.0 → 70.1 → 71.4; GPT-2 24.8 → 55.7 → 62.3. — [same source, Table 2](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01183)
- What the local dominant directions encode: "local dominant directions for the clusters of punctuations and stop words carry structural and syntactic information about the sentences"; "tense is more dominant in verb representations than sense-level semantic information" — after removing verb-cluster top PCs, different-tense/same-meaning pairs become *closer* than same-tense/different-meaning pairs for BERT and RoBERTa (Table 4: BERT DT-SM 14.87 → 10.32 vs ST-DM 14.24 → 10.50). The paper does not generalize beyond these two cases. — [same source, §4, Table 4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01183)
- A NeurIPS 2022 ML Reproducibility Challenge report replicated the method ("[Re] A Cluster-based Approach for Improving Isotropy in Contextual Embedding Space"). — [NeurIPS 2022 listing](https://nips.cc/virtual/2022/poster/56099)

**Cai, Huang, Bian & Church (ICLR 2021), "Isotropy in the Contextual Embedding Space: Clusters and Manifolds"**
- Abstract (confirmed): prior work "demonstrate[s] a strong anisotropic space" where most vectors fall in a narrow cone; the authors argue isotropy "does exist in the space" from a different angle, and "identify isolated clusters and low dimensional manifolds in the contextual embedding space", with tools to analyse them. Authors: Xingyu Cai, Jiaji Huang, Yuchen Bian, Kenneth Church (Baidu). 181 citations on Semantic Scholar as of this search. — [ICLR 2021 poster page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2021/poster/3079); [ML Anthology listing](https://mlanthology.org/iclr/2021/cai2021iclr-isotropy/); [OpenReview forum](https://openreview.net/forum?id=xYGNO86OWDH)
- Secondary description: Rajaee & Pilehvar characterise Cai et al.'s claim as local isotropy inside clusters measured by cosine similarity, and report their own GPT-2 result as contradicting it. — [Rajaee & Pilehvar 2021, §3](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01183)

### Inferences
- "Centering" in this literature has two separate roles: (i) global centering removes a shared offset that is mostly a frequency/positional artefact and makes cosine similarity meaningful; (ii) per-cluster centering (Rajaee & Pilehvar) is a way to *measure* isotropy locally and to remove *local* nuisance directions. Nobody in this strand reads the local top PC as the cluster's "meaning"; the evidence is that the top local PCs are tense, syntax, stop-word structure, and frequency.
- The jump from I(W) = 0.010 (whole space zero-meaned) to 0.262/0.603 (20 clusters each zero-meaned) in BERT/RoBERTa is direct quantitative evidence that contextual embedding spaces are better described as a union of locally-centered blobs than as one blob — the "local origin" picture has empirical support, but for *geometry*, not for semantic eigenvectors.
- Because the mean and top PCs cancel in b − a, offset-based analogy accuracy is nearly unchanged by centering (Mu et al.); any proposal that depends on *where the origin is* inside a cluster is therefore testing something the offset literature was blind to.

### Gaps
- Cai et al. 2021 full text: OpenReview blocks automated fetches with a browser check, and Semantic Scholar lists no open-access PDF. I could not verify from the primary source the specific details (which models; that shifting each cluster by its mean yields local isotropy; "swiss-roll"-shaped low-dimensional manifolds tied to token position/frequency; local-intrinsic-dimension numbers). Treat any such details as unconfirmed unless the report writer can open the OpenReview PDF (`https://openreview.net/pdf?id=xYGNO86OWDH`).
- Arora et al. 2016's "common direction" is their isotropy *assumption* and the partition-function concentration lemma; I did not extract a specific number for how far real embeddings deviate from it beyond Mu et al.'s I(W) values.

## Key question 3: Concept directions found by linear algebra on word subsets (gender direction, semantic axes), how they are defined and validated, and what removing them does

### Takeaway
The standard recipe is "take curated antonym/counterfactual word *pairs*, difference them, and either average the differences (SemAxis, Kozlowski et al., Park et al. 2023) or take the top principal component of the differences (Bolukbasi et al.)"; the axis is validated by projecting held-out words and correlating with human ratings (ρ ≈ 0.5–0.9 for gender, 0.4–0.6 for class). The PCA-of-pair-differences version is the closest existing thing to "an eigenvector encoding a relation", but it is PCA of *differences*, not of a raw cluster, and Gonen & Goldberg showed that removing such a direction leaves the concept almost fully recoverable from the rest of the space.

### Cited Findings

**Bolukbasi et al. (NeurIPS 2016) — gender direction = top PC of pair differences**
- *Gloss: take ten he/she-type pairs, subtract each pair, and find the one direction that best explains how those ten difference vectors vary; that direction is "gender".* Pairs: she–he, her–his, woman–man, Mary–John, herself–himself, daughter–son, mother–father, gal–guy, girl–boy, female–male. Procedure (§5.1): "we took the ten gender pair difference vectors and computed its principal components… there is a single direction that explains the majority of variance in these vectors. The first eigenvalue is significantly larger than the rest", compared against 1,000 draws of ten random unit vectors in 300-d, which show a "gradual and uniform" decrease. In the general algorithm (§6, Step 1) each defining set is centered on its own mean μ_i before the SVD. Embedding: word2vec 300-d trained on Google News (3 million words/phrases), filtered to 26,377 lowercase words. — [Bolukbasi, Chang, Zou, Saligrama & Kalai 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)
- Validation: projection of occupation words onto the she–he direction correlates with crowd-rated stereotypicality at Spearman ρ = 0.51; projections are consistent across embeddings (word2vec vs GloVe ρ = 0.81). Of 150 analogies generated along the she–he direction, 72 were judged gender-appropriate and 29 (≈19%) stereotyped by ≥5 of 10 workers; after hard debiasing, 6%. Debiasing by "neutralize and equalize" did not degrade benchmarks (RG 62.3 → 62.4; WS 54.5 → 54.1; analogy 57.0 → 57.0). — [same source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.06520)

**Gonen & Goldberg (NAACL 2019) — "Lipstick on a Pig": the direction measures the concept but does not contain it**
- After removing the gender direction (hard-debias) or training without it (GN-GloVe), k-means (k = 2) on the 1,000 most-biased words still aligns with gender 92.5% (hard-debias; 99.9% before) and 85.6% (GN-GloVe; 100% before). An RBF-SVM trained on 1,000 words predicts gender of 4,000 others at 88.88% (hard-debias; 98.25% before) and 96.53% (GN-GloVe; 98.65% before). Bias-by-neighbours correlates with original bias at Pearson 0.686 / 0.736. Conclusion: the gender direction "provides a way to measure the gender-association of a word, but does not determine it"; the effect of projection-based debiasing is "mostly hiding the bias, not removing it". — [Gonen & Goldberg 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.03862)

**Iterative null-space projection (Ravfogel et al., ACL 2020) and partial projection (Dev & Phillips 2019)**
- *Gloss: instead of one hand-picked direction, train a linear classifier to predict the attribute, project the data onto that classifier's null-space, and repeat until no linear classifier can find it.* INLP trains a linear classifier for the protected attribute, computes its null-space projection, applies it, and iterates; the paper reports that a linear gender classifier on GloVe drops from 100% to 49.3% after INLP in one experiment, while an RBF classifier can still recover gender at 85.0% ("this is expected", since INLP only removes linearly decodable information). — [Ravfogel, Elazar, Gonen, Twiton & Goldberg 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07667)

**Semantic axes: SemAxis (An, Kwak & Ahn, ACL 2018)**
- *Gloss: pick a set of "positive-pole" words and a set of "negative-pole" words, average each set, subtract, and score any word by its cosine with that axis.* Formally V_axis = V+ − V− where V+ = (1/n)Σ v+_i and V− = (1/m)Σ v−_j; score(w) = cos(v_w, V_axis). Any antonymous pair can define an axis ("clean vs dirty", "respectful vs disrespectful"). Practical challenges named: small corpora and choosing pole words. — [An, Kwak & Ahn 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05521)

**"The Geometry of Culture" (Kozlowski, Taddy & Evans, American Sociological Review 2019)**
- *Gloss: a cultural dimension such as rich–poor is the average of several antonym-pair differences; a word's position on it is the cosine of its vector with that axis.* "Cultural dimensions are calculated by simply taking the mean of all word pair differences that approximate a given dimension"; "averaging between six and ten pairs of words for each dimension… produces a closer approximation… than any one word-pair"; a word's association is the orthogonal projection of its normalized vector, "equivalent to their cosine similarity". Induced dimensions (gender, class, …) are argued to be approximately orthogonal because random vectors in high dimension are nearly orthogonal. — [Kozlowski, Taddy & Evans 2019 (arXiv 1803.09288)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09288)
- Validation: a survey of 398 MTurk respondents (2016–17) rated 59 words on gender/class/race scales; Pearson correlation between survey rating and embedding projection on the gender dimension is 0.88 (Google News word2vec), 0.90 (GloVe Common Crawl), 0.87 (fastText Wikipedia), 0.78 (Google N-grams); class 0.40–0.60; race 0.70 (Google News), 0.42, 0.49, 0.17 (N-grams). For word pairs with significantly different survey ratings, the embedding orders them correctly above 80% (often above 90%) within most domains, 51.8% for clothing items (weak racial associations) and 100% for first names. — [same source, Tables 2–3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09288)

### Inferences
- All of these define the axis from *pairs that differ in exactly the target attribute*, and all project onto it with a dot product/cosine. None defines the axis from the spread of a single cluster of related words. Bolukbasi's PCA step is the sole place an eigen-decomposition is used, and its input is the 10 centered pair-difference vectors, not the word vectors themselves — the "eigenvector" is the common direction of a set of offsets.
- Validation is always external: correlate projections with human ratings, or show that the axis reproduces known orderings. Variance explained by the PC is used only as a sanity check against a random baseline.
- Gonen & Goldberg's result is a general warning: a direction that *correlates* with a concept (and hence can be used to read it off) typically does not *contain* the concept; the information is spread through the neighbourhood structure.

### Gaps
- Bolukbasi's exact percentage of variance explained by the first component (Figure 6) is not in the text, only "majority of variance".
- Dev & Phillips 2019 ("Attenuating bias in word vectors", partial/soft projection) was not fetched; omitted rather than described from memory.
- SemAxis' validation numbers (sentiment lexicon correlations, k-nearest-neighbour pole expansion) were not extracted.

## Key question 4: The linear representation hypothesis in LLMs — causal inner product, hierarchical/categorical geometry, superposition and sparse autoencoders, and non-linear (circular) features

### Takeaway
In LLMs, concepts are (approximately) linear directions, but the Euclidean dot product is the wrong geometry: Park et al. show concept directions become orthogonal only under a "causal inner product" (whitening by the unembedding covariance), and under it hierarchical sub-concepts are literally orthogonal offsets from the parent concept vector (mammal − animal ⊥ animal) with categorical siblings forming a simplex. Separately, Anthropic/OpenAI sparse-autoencoder work finds millions of near-linear feature directions — far more than the dimension count — using dictionary learning rather than PCA, with the caveat (Engels et al. 2024) that some features are genuinely multi-dimensional (days of the week form a circle).

### Cited Findings

**Park, Choe & Veitch (ICML 2024) — "The Linear Representation Hypothesis and the Geometry of LLMs"**
- *Gloss: three different things people mean by "linear representation" (a concept is a direction; a concept can be read off by a linear probe; a concept can be changed by adding a vector) are unified, but only after replacing the ordinary dot product with one learned from the model.* The unembedding space is identified only up to an invertible affine map (softmax is shift-invariant), so "nothing in the training objective picks out a natural inner product" and Euclidean similarity "is not guaranteed to be semantically meaningful". Definition 3.1: a *causal inner product* is one under which causally separable concepts are orthogonal; Theorem 3.4 shows one valid choice is ⟨γ̄, γ̄′⟩_C = γ̄ᵀ Cov(γ)⁻¹ γ̄′ (inverse covariance of the unembedding vectors), with the caveat "we do not have a principle for picking out a unique choice". Concept directions are estimated as the mean of counterfactual unembedding differences γ̃_W = (1/n)Σ[γ(y_i(1)) − γ(y_i(0))], normalized. Experiments: LLaMA-2 7B (32,000 tokens, 4,096-d), 27 concepts (22 from BATS 3.0, e.g. male→female with 11 pairs, lower→upper 34 pairs; 4 language pairs; frequent→infrequent). Under the causal inner product |⟨γ̄_W, γ̄_Z⟩_C| is near zero for most concept pairs with block structure by semantic grouping; counterfactual differences align with the concept direction far better than 100K random word-pair differences (exception: thing→part); adding α·(male→female) with α from 0 to 0.4 moves the top-1 prediction from "king" to "queen". — [Park, Choe & Veitch 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658)

**Park, Choe, Jiang & Veitch (ICLR 2025 oral) — "The Geometry of Categorical and Hierarchical Concepts in LLMs"**
- *Gloss: a concept with no natural opposite (is_animal) is a vector; a category's members (mammal, bird, fish, …) form a polytope; and a sub-concept's vector minus its parent's vector is orthogonal to the parent — i.e. sub-concepts are offsets from a parent "origin".* Estimator: whiten unembeddings g(y) = Cov(γ)^{-1/2}(γ(y) − E[γ]); for attribute w with token set Y(w), g̃_w = Cov(g_w)† E(g_w) / ‖·‖ (an LDA-style direction, Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage because dimension ≫ sample size), then ℓ̄_w = (g̃_wᵀ E(g_w)) g̃_w; the plain mean estimator is noted to have high variance. Theorem 8 (hierarchical orthogonality): (a) ℓ̄_w ⊥ ℓ̄_z − ℓ̄_w for z ≺ w (e.g. ℓ̄_mammal − ℓ̄_animal ⊥ ℓ̄_animal); (b) ℓ̄_w ⊥ ℓ̄_{z1} − ℓ̄_{z0} for siblings z0, z1 under w; (c) ℓ̄_{w1} − ℓ̄_{w0} ⊥ ℓ̄_{z1} − ℓ̄_{z0}; (d) ℓ̄_{w1} − ℓ̄_{w0} ⊥ ℓ̄_{w2} − ℓ̄_{w1} down a chain. Definition 7: the polytope representation of a categorical concept is the convex hull of its elements' vectors; Proposition 9: if every joint distribution over the k attributes is attainable, the k vectors form a (k−1)-simplex. — [Park, Choe, Jiang & Veitch 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01506)
- Experiments: Gemma-2B (LLaMA-3-8B in Appendix F, "largely analogous"); WordNet, 593 noun + 364 verb synsets with ≥50 vocabulary words = 957 concepts ("900+"). Cosine-similarity heatmaps reproduce WordNet parent–child and sibling block structure (shuffled control ≈ orthogonal); child–parent cosine is close to 0 for real embeddings (the paper notes a cosine of −0.2 is "highly nontrivial" in high dimensions). Table 1: adding the parent direction changes the parent logit difference (plant→animal 5.13 ± 1.17; fluid→solid 9.83 ± 1.11) but not the child's (mammal→reptile −0.06 ± 1.22; crystal→food 0.38 ± 1.54). Figure 6: mammal, bird, fish vertices form a triangle orthogonal to the projected ℓ̄_animal arrow. Stated limits: representations are expected to be "approximate, noisy"; "only works for the final layer representation"; the authors do not explain *why* linear representations exist. — [same source](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01506v3)

**Superposition and sparse-autoencoder features (Anthropic, OpenAI)**
- *Gloss: a model can store far more "features" than it has dimensions by using nearly-orthogonal directions that are rarely active at the same time; a sparse autoencoder is a second network trained to re-express each activation as a few of those directions, which is how the features are found.* Elhage et al. 2022, "Toy Models of Superposition" (Transformer Circuits thread, sections "Background & Motivation", "Demonstrating Superposition") is the source for superposition; I could only retrieve its headings (see Gaps). — [Elhage et al. 2022](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)
- Templeton et al. 2024 (Scaling Monosemanticity), as summarised by Anthropic: features were extracted from the middle layer of Claude 3 Sonnet with "dictionary learning"; concepts "are spread across many neurons, and each neuron takes part in many concepts"; a distance measure based on shared neurons shows related features near each other (Golden Gate Bridge near Alcatraz, Ghirardelli Square, the 1906 earthquake, Hitchcock's *Vertigo*; "inner conflict" near breakups and "catch-22"); amplifying the Golden Gate Bridge feature causally changed outputs ("Golden Gate Claude"). Limits: the features found are "a small subset" of what the model learned; a full dictionary would be "cost-prohibitive". — [Anthropic, "Mapping the Mind of a Large Language Model" (May 2024)](https://www.anthropic.com/research/mapping-mind-language-model); full paper: [Templeton et al. 2024](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/index.html)
- Gao et al. 2024 (OpenAI): a 16-million-latent TopK sparse autoencoder trained on 40 billion tokens of GPT-4 residual-stream activations (layer ≈5/6 of depth; GPT-2 small d_model = 768 at layer 8); the encoder keeps only the k largest latents; "only 7% of latents are dead"; reconstruction MSE follows power laws in compute and in latent count; evaluations include downstream KL/cross-entropy, 1-d logistic probes on 61 datasets, explanation precision/recall, and ablation sparsity. Limits: "many random activations of GPT-4 features are not yet adequately monosemantic"; scaling breaks down as k → d_model. — [Gao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04093)

**Not all features are one-dimensional (Engels, Michaud, Liao, Gurnee & Tegmark 2024)**
- *Gloss: some concepts are arranged on a circle or other low-dimensional shape rather than a line; days of the week are a wheel.* A feature is "irreducibly" multi-dimensional if no rotation/translation makes it separable or a mixture. Method: train SAEs, cluster dictionary elements by cosine similarity, reconstruct activations from each cluster, inspect PCA projections. Findings: circular representations of days of the week, months, and 20th-century years in GPT-2-small (layer 7) and Mistral-7B; Llama-3-8B circles appear in PCA and intervention experiments. Interventions on "Two days from Monday is" / "Four months from January is": Llama-3-8B 29/49 weekdays and 143/144 months correct; Mistral-7B 31/49 and 125/144; GPT-2 8/49 and 10/144 (GPT-2 has the circles but does not use them for this task). The authors describe the circles as "linear" (inside a low-dimensional linear subspace) but not one-dimensional, and call this "a significant refinement to the simple one-dimensional linear representation hypothesis". — [Engels et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14860)

### Inferences
- Park et al. 2024 is the strongest published support for the *spirit* of the learner's proposal: within the "animal" category, sub-concept vectors are offsets from the parent vector, orthogonal to it, and siblings form a simplex — a "sub-origin" picture. But three things differ from "PCA on a centered cluster": the directions are estimated from labelled word sets via an LDA-type estimator, not from unsupervised variance; the orthogonality is only true under a whitened (causal) inner product, not raw Euclidean geometry; and it holds for the final-layer unembedding space, not for arbitrary layers or static word vectors.
- The SAE literature implies that the number of meaningful directions vastly exceeds the dimension, so the top eigenvector of any cluster's covariance is an average over many superposed features; dictionary learning exists precisely because PCA does not separate them.
- Engels et al. show that even for a clean, closed set of related words (Monday…Sunday) the structure can be a 2-D circle, so a one-dimensional "lineage" axis is not the only shape a related-word cluster can take.

## Key question 5: Does "center a cluster of related words on its own mean/medoid and run PCA, reading the top eigenvector as the relation" appear anywhere? Closest matches

### Takeaway
No paper I found does exactly this (centered-cluster PCA → top eigenvector = semantic relation). The nearest matches, in decreasing closeness, are: Rajaee & Pilehvar 2021 (k-means clusters, zero-mean each, per-cluster PCA — but they *remove* the top PCs as nuisance and find they encode tense/syntax), Bolukbasi et al. 2016 (top PC of ten centered *pair differences* = gender direction, validated externally), Park et al. 2024 (sub-concept vectors as orthogonal offsets from the parent vector under a whitened inner product, estimated with LDA from labelled word sets), Ethayarajh 2019 (first PC of one word's contextual vectors explains <5% of variance after anisotropy correction), and Mu et al. 2018 (global centering + top-PC removal).

### Cited Findings
- Rajaee & Pilehvar: "clustered the space using k-means and measured isotropy after making each cluster zero-mean"; nulls out "the top PCs of every cluster … separately"; the top local PCs encode "structural and syntactic information" (punctuation/stop-word clusters) and tense (verb clusters). — [Rajaee & Pilehvar 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01183)
- Bolukbasi et al.: PCA of ten pair-difference vectors, each normalized; "a single direction that explains the majority of variance"; in the general algorithm each defining set is centered on its own mean μ_i before the SVD. — [Bolukbasi et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.06520)
- Park et al. 2024: Theorem 8(a) ℓ̄_w ⊥ (ℓ̄_z − ℓ̄_w) for subordinate z; vectors estimated by an LDA-like estimator on whitened unembeddings of the words in each WordNet synset; "only works for the final layer". — [Park, Choe, Jiang & Veitch 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01506v3)
- Ethayarajh 2019: maximum explainable variance σ₁²/Σσᵢ² of a single word's occurrence matrix is below 5% in every layer after subtracting the random-word baseline. — [Ethayarajh 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.00512)
- Mu et al.: top PCs of the centered static embedding matrix are dominated by a shared, frequency-related subspace of ~8 dimensions out of 300; removing them helps similarity but leaves analogies unchanged. — [Mu, Bhat & Viswanath 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01417)
- Kozlowski et al. / SemAxis / Park et al. 2023: axis = mean of pair differences; validated by correlation with human ratings (gender 0.78–0.90) or by orthogonality/steering tests. — [Kozlowski et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09288); [An et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05521); [Park, Choe & Veitch 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658)

### Inferences
- The literature's consistent lesson is that the direction of *greatest variance* inside a set of word vectors is, by default, a nuisance direction (frequency, tense, syntax, positional structure), whereas semantic relation directions are found by *contrasting labelled pairs* (differences) and then averaging or taking the top PC of those differences. A learner's cluster PCA would therefore need either a difference-based construction (pairs of father/uncle-type words that differ only in lineage) or an external validation step (projection of held-out words correlating with a lineage judgement) to be interpretable in the way these papers are.
- The "sub-origin" intuition is not naive: Park et al. 2024 formalize exactly a parent-vector origin with orthogonal child offsets, and Rajaee & Pilehvar show per-cluster centering is the right way to even measure the local geometry. What is unsupported is the step "top eigenvector of the centered cluster = the semantic relation": in every case examined, that eigenvector carried something else.
- Any such analysis in a modern LLM should also expect (a) the Euclidean inner product to be the wrong metric unless the space is whitened (Park et al. 2023), (b) many superposed features per direction (SAE literature), and (c) possible multi-dimensional (circular) structure (Engels et al.).
