# Semantics of English kinship terms as a feature space, and what embeddings recover

Scope note: this covers (1) the classical componential/feature analyses of English kin terms and the psychological-scaling evidence for which features are salient, (2) Kemp & Regier's communicative-efficiency account, (3) what word-embedding and neural-network evaluations have shown for kinship (Google analogy "family" set, BATS, Hinton 1986, a 2024 French distributional study), and (4) what embeddings should be expected to do differently from the feature table. Linear-algebra and anisotropy literature are deliberately excluded.

Primary texts were read directly where possible (Hinton 1986 via OCR of the scanned PDF; Kemp & Regier 2012, BATS, Tanguy et al. 2024, Bolukbasi et al. 2016, Horn 2017, Hallam et al. 2025 via PDF text extraction). Wallace & Atkins 1960 and Romney & D'Andrade 1964 are paywalled; their tables are reproduced from two secondary sources that explicitly adapt the original figures (Boster's cognitive-anthropology chapter, and Borgatti's analytictech course page). Everything not verified against a primary text is flagged.

## Key Question 1: Componential analysis of English kin terms — the "expected" feature table

### Takeaway
Two classic analyses of the 15 core consanguineal English terms agree on three underlying dimensions — sex of referent, generation, and lineality — but differ on how to carve generation and lineality. Wallace & Atkins (1960) use absolute generation (+2…−2) and three degrees of lineality (lineal / colineal / ablineal); Romney & D'Andrade (1964) use generation *distance* (0, 1, 2) plus a reciprocity (ascending vs descending) feature and only two degrees of lineality (direct / collateral). Triads data from 116 and 155 subjects correlated .86/.84 with the Romney–D'Andrade model vs .33/.40 with Wallace–Atkins, so the psychologically salient structure pairs reciprocals (grandfather~grandson, father~son, uncle~nephew) and separates direct from collateral lines, rather than ordering terms on an absolute generation axis.

### Cited Findings

**Method and lineage of the approach**
- Wallace & Atkins (1960) described componential analysis (as propounded by Goodenough 1956 and Lounsbury 1956) as five steps: record a complete set of kin terms; define each by its kin-types (e.g., MoFa, MoBrSo); identify two or more feature dimensions whose values are signified by the terms; define each term by its feature values; state the semantic structure of the whole set. They started from "the consanguineal core terms" of English. — [Boster, "Categories and Cognitive Anthropology", §4, pp. 16–18](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)
- Citation: Wallace, A. F. C. & Atkins, J. (1960). "The Meaning of Kinship Terms." *American Anthropologist* 62(1): 58–80. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227998257_The_Meaning_of_Kinship_Terms1); [Boster Table 3 caption](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)
- Citation: Romney, A. K. & D'Andrade, R. G. (1964). "Cognitive Aspects of English Kin Terms." *American Anthropologist* 66(3), Part 2: 146–170 (the "Transcultural Studies in Cognition" special issue). Volume/issue numbers vary across citing sources (66(2) in Boster; 66(3) per the Wiley DOI; some secondary sources wrongly give vol. 67). — [Wiley DOI 10.1525/aa.1964.66.3.02a00870](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1525/aa.1964.66.3.02a00870); [Boster Table 4 caption](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)
- Goodenough-style intensional definition of *uncle*: "any kinsman by blood or marriage who is simultaneously (a) male, (b) two degrees of genealogical distance from ego, (c) not lineal, (d) senior generation, (e) no marital tie except in the senior generation"; changing one attribute yields *aunt* (sex), *great-uncle* (distance), *grandfather* (lineality), *nephew* (generation). — [Encyclopedia.com, "Componential Analysis"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/sociology-and-social-reform/sociology-general-terms-and-concepts/componential-analysis)

**Wallace & Atkins 1960 — dimensions and definitions**
- "According to Wallace and Atkins (1960), the male terms in the American kinship system are completely described by their values along two dimensions: generation and lineality." (Sex is the third dimension once female terms are added.) — [Borgatti, analytictech "Componential Analysis of American Kinship Terms"](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- Definitions: "Lineals are persons who are direct ancestors or descendents of ego. Collineals are nonlineals all of whose ancestors include or are included in all ancestors of ego. Ablineals are consanguineal relatives who are neither lineals nor collineals." — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- Male-term grid (generation × lineality): +2 GrFa (lineal); +1 Fa (lineal), Un (colineal); 0 Br (colineal), Co (ablineal); −1 So (lineal), Ne (colineal); −2 GrSo (lineal). — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- Full-term version (adapted from W&A Figure 1): rows +2…−2 by sex, columns lineal / colineal / ablineal, with grandfather/grandmother, father/mother, son/daughter, grandson/granddaughter lineal; uncle/aunt, brother/sister, nephew/niece colineal; cousin ablineal (not split by sex). — [Boster, Table 3](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)

**Romney & D'Andrade 1964 — dimensions and definitions**
- "According to Romney & D'Andrade (1964), the male terms are best described in three dimensions: generation (3 values), lineality (2 values), role reciprocal (2 values)." The male grid: direct/+recip: GrFa (2), Fa (1); direct/−recip: GrSo (2), So (1); collateral/+recip: Un (1); collateral/−recip: Ne (1); generation 0: Brother (direct), Cousin (collateral). — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- Full-term version (adapted from R&D Figure 3): columns direct (male/female) vs collateral (male/female); rows "two generations different" {+: grandfather, grandmother; −: grandson, granddaughter}, "one generation different" {+: father, mother, uncle, aunt; −: son, daughter, nephew, niece}, "same generation" {brother, sister | cousin}. — [Boster, Table 4](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)
- "the principal differences with Wallace and Atkins (1960) are that there are only two degrees of lineality (direct and collateral) rather than three (lineal, colineal, and ablineal) and that generational terms that are reciprocals of one another (e.g., grandmother, granddaughter; mother, daughter) are treated as sharing the feature value of being one or two generations away from ego, rather than distinguishing kin terms by their absolute generation." — [Boster, pp. 19–20](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)
- R&D's underlying notation: "m" male, "f" female, "a" either sex, "0" sibling link, "+" parent link, "−" child link, "=" marriage link, "/" encloses reciprocal readings; e.g. mother's brother's son = "a+f0m−m". — [Boster, p. 19](https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/cda/*boster.coganth.pdf)
- R&D's reduced definitions (their p. 148): Grandfather a+m+m / a+f+m; Brother a0m; Uncle a+m0m / a+f0m; Nephew a0m−m / a0f−m. "Romney and D'Andrade (1964) uses about 10 features to cover 15 core English kinship terms." — [Baik & Chae 2010, PACLIC 24](https://aclanthology.org/Y10-1039.pdf)

**The triads test and which model is psychologically real**
- Design: a triad test over the 8 male terms (56 triads; each pair occurs 6 times); subjects pick the term "most different"; each model's predicted choice frequencies come from city-block distances on its feature grid. Example triad {GrFa, GrSo, Fa}: W–A distances GrFa–Fa 1, GrFa–GrSo 4, GrSo–Fa 3 → predicts 100% choose GrSo; R–D distances GrFa–GrSo 1, GrFa–Fa 1, GrSo–Fa 2 → predicts 50/50 between Fa and GrSo. — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- Results: correlation between predicted and observed triad frequencies — Experiment 1 (N=116): Wallace–Atkins .33, Romney–D'Andrade .86; Experiment 2 (N=155): .40 vs .84. "If [one uses] a regression technique to find the right weights for each dimension, both models become more accurate, but the Romney-D'Andrade model still fits considerably better." — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- Subjects in the first experiment were 116 high-school students. — [search summary of secondary sources; see Gaps]
- Independent summary: Romney & D'Andrade "introduced free-listing … and triadic similarity judgments as a way to estimate the cognitive structure for terms in a domain"; the similarity data fit the three-dimensional Romney–D'Andrade model better than the two-dimensional Wallace–Atkins model. — [NAS biographical memoir of A. Kimball Romney](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Romney-A.-Kimball.pdf)
- Individual variation: Romney & Wexler re-analysed the triads per respondent with MDS of inter-respondent agreement; a core group follows R–D closely, and about 30% of respondents resolve R–D's tied triads consistently in the W–A direction, but "there do not appear to be any persons whose answers strongly resemble the Wallace-Atkins model." — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)

**A modern "etic" feature coding used cross-linguistically (useful as a neutral answer key)**
- Hallam, Kirby, Jordan & Smith (CogSci 2025) code every kin type over five generations with six features "compiled from the anthropological literature on kinship (Goodenough, 1956; Murdock, 1949; Greenberg, 2005)": Generation (G+2, G+1, G0, G−1, G−2), Gender (woman, man), Lineality (lineal, collateral — "Whether relative is in Ego's direct lineage"), Gender of connecting relative (woman/man/NA), Relative age (elder/younger/NA), Speaker gender. Only consanguineal kin were included. — [Hallam et al. 2025, Table 1](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1fg7b7hn)
- English *uncle* collapses four etic kin types ('mother's elder brother', 'mother's younger brother', 'father's elder brother', 'father's younger brother'); "in English, the term grandson is compositional". — [Hallam et al. 2025](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1fg7b7hn)

Note that brother/sister are *colineal* for W&A but *direct* for R&D, and cousin is the sole ablineal term for W&A and the sole same-generation collateral for R&D.

Predicted groupings if a cluster "works":
- Sex: a binary split cross-cutting everything ({father, son, grandfather, brother, uncle, nephew, grandson} vs female counterparts).
- Generation as an ordered axis (W&A): grandparents > parents/uncle/aunt > ego's generation > children/nephew/niece > grandchildren.
- Lineal vs collateral: {grandfather, father, son, grandson, (brother per R&D)} vs {uncle, nephew, cousin, (brother per W&A)}.
- Reciprocal pairing (R&D, the empirically favoured structure): {grandfather, grandson}, {father, son}, {uncle, nephew} sit close; distance from ego (0/1/2) rather than signed generation is the salient scale.

### Inferences
- The three analyses (W&A, R&D, Hallam's etic coding) converge on sex, generation and lineality as the universal dimensions of the English core set; the disagreement is only about (a) whether generation is signed-absolute or unsigned-distance-plus-direction, and (b) whether siblings go with lineals or collaterals. A report can therefore treat sex, generation and lineality as the "answer key" and treat the sibling placement and the reciprocity question as diagnostic of *which* model an embedding resembles.
- Under R&D, the term space is nearly a product of three small factors (sex 2 × distance 3 × lineality 2, with reciprocity only applying at distance ≥ 1), i.e., a very low-dimensional, almost factorial code — exactly what a local PCA would be expected to recover as a few near-orthogonal axes.
- For a learner that uses reciprocal structure (the R&D pattern), father↔son should be closer than father↔grandfather; for a learner using absolute generation (W&A), father↔grandfather and father↔son should be equidistant on the generation axis. This is a concrete, testable discriminator.

### Gaps
- I could not access the original Wallace & Atkins 1960 or Romney & D'Andrade 1964 texts (JSTOR/Wiley paywalled; Wiley returned 403). The tables above are from two secondary sources that state they are adapted from the originals' Figure 1 and Figure 3 respectively; the per-cell values agree between the two secondary sources for male terms. The W&A letter codes often quoted in textbooks (a1/a2 sex, b1–b5 generation, c1–c3 lineality) were not verified and are omitted.
- Cousin's generation in W&A: analytictech places Co at "same" generation; Boster's reproduction shows cousin spanning the ablineal column without a clear generation row, and some textbook renderings leave cousin unmarked for generation. Unresolved; the report should say "same generation (or unmarked)".
- Boster's extracted Table 3 reads "father father" for the +1 lineal row — almost certainly a text-extraction error for "father mother"; treated as such.
- The "116 high school students" detail comes only from a search-engine summary of a secondary source, not from a page I read.
- The Sanday 1968 "psychological reality of American-English kinship terms" paper (American Anthropologist 70) exists but I could not read it.

## Key Question 2: Kemp & Regier 2012 — kinship as a near-optimal simplicity/informativeness trade-off

### Takeaway
Kemp & Regier formalise every kin-term system as a partition of a 56-person family tree over five generations, measure its complexity as the minimum number of rules in a small logical language (English = 15 rules from primitives like PARENT, CHILD, FEMALE, MALE), and its communicative cost in bits; attested systems in 487 languages sit near the Pareto frontier of these two quantities. This is good evidence that the kinship domain has a compact, rule-describable structure built from a handful of primitives (sex, parent/child link, relative age).

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Kemp, C. & Regier, T. (2012). "Kinship categories across languages reflect general communicative principles." *Science* 336(6084): 1049–1054. — [Berkeley LCLab PDF](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)
- Summary sentence: "The competing principles of simplicity and informativeness help to explain which kin classification systems are found in the languages of the world." "We show computationally that kin classification systems in the world's languages achieve a near-optimal tradeoff between these two competing principles." — [Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)
- Kin types covered: "grandparents, parents, aunts, uncles, siblings, children, nieces, nephews, and grandchildren" — "the largest set of kin types for which we have kin naming data and for which our analyses are computationally tractable"; cousins are excluded from the main analysis (handled in supplementary material). Each category "corresponds to a subset of the 56 individuals in Figure 2A"; Alice's own tree has 24 individuals. — [Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)
- Data: Murdock's cross-cultural data set "includes kin classification systems for 566 languages"; after cleaning, "complete systems for 487 languages in total. These systems include 410 distinct types … these 410 types represent only a tiny fraction of the 10^55 systems that are possible in theory." (This explains the 566 vs 487 discrepancy between press reports.) — [Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)
- Simplicity: "the complexity of a system corresponds to the length of its shortest description in this language." Primitives include "features like FEMALE(·) and relations like PARENT(·, ·)" (Figure 3A also lists MALE, CHILD, YOUNGER as used in the extracted rule listings); combination rules are conjunction/disjunction, relative product (e.g., grandmother = mother ∘ PARENT), inverse, symmetric closure, transitive closure. "the complexity of the English system (Figure 2C) is 15, and the complexity of the Northern Paiute system (Figure 2D) is 24." — [Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)
- Informativeness: cost of referring to individual i is c_i = −log2(p_i / Σ_{z_j = z_i} p_j) bits; communicative cost = expected cost over need probabilities p_i, averaged over a female and a male speaker (Alice and Bob). Need probabilities were estimated from the Corpus of Contemporary American English and the German Reference Corpus. — [Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)
- Results: ~71,000 candidate categories enumerated by applying the rules up to three times; "The majority of attested systems (black circles) are found near the optimal frontier"; also holds for the 8.3 × 10^8 systems built from categories appearing in >2 attested systems, and for sub-analyses of grandparents, grandchildren, siblings, mother+aunts, father+uncles, children+niblings; "frequent systems should tend to lie closer to the optimal frontier than rare systems" is supported. — [Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF)
- Critique: Sperber notes the analysis treats any partition of genealogical relations as a possible terminology and does not model the internal logic of terminologies. — [Sperber, "Is kinship back?"](https://cognitionandculture.net/blogs/dan-sperber/is-kinship-back)
- Follow-ups: Anand & Regier (2023) argue need distributions are culture-specific (Hindi vs English). — [Anand & Regier 2023](https://lclab.berkeley.edu/papers/anand-regier-2023.pdf). Hallam et al. (2025) extend the efficient-communication view to show semantic features are "encoded hierarchically; more simple and more informative features are weighted highest" in form–meaning mappings. — [Hallam et al. 2025](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1fg7b7hn)

### Inferences
- Kemp & Regier never say "low-dimensional" in so many words; the report should phrase the point as: the English system is fully generated by 15 rules over sex and parent/child primitives, i.e., it is a compact product-like code, which is why a feature table with three dimensions suffices.
- The need-probability finding (parents are referred to more than children because everyone has parents) is a principled reason to expect frequency asymmetries across generations in corpora, which will in turn affect embedding quality for low-frequency terms (nephew, niece, grandson).

### Gaps
- The exact primitive list in Figure 3A was not fully recoverable from the PDF text (figure text was partly glyph-encoded); FEMALE, MALE, PARENT, CHILD and YOUNGER are confirmed from extracted rule lines, others not.

## Key Question 3: Kinship in embedding evaluations (Google analogy "family", BATS, Hinton 1986, distributional studies)

### Takeaway
Standard analogy benchmarks test only the *sex* dimension inside kinship: the Google "family" category is 23 male:female pairs (506 questions) on which word2vec scores ~78–80%, and BATS has no kinship category beyond a male:female set. No benchmark I found tests generation (father→grandfather) or lineality (father→uncle) offsets. The strongest direct evidence that distributed representations discover generation and branch features is Hinton's 1986 family-tree network, where individual hidden units came to encode nationality, generation (a three-valued unit) and family branch, and none encoded sex because the relation terms made it redundant. A 2024 French study shows SGNS embeddings of 25 kin terms are extremely cohesive and that componential feature differences explain 11–38% of cosine-similarity variance, with gender the strongest single feature.

### Cited Findings

**Google analogy test set — the "family" category**
- The questions-words.txt file has 14 sections; the ": family" section contains 506 questions built from 23 pairs, all of the form male:female: boy:girl, brother:sister, brothers:sisters, dad:mom, father:mother, grandfather:grandmother, grandpa:grandma, grandson:granddaughter, groom:bride, he:she, his:her, husband:wife, king:queen, man:woman, nephew:niece, policeman:policewoman, prince:princess, son:daughter, sons:daughters, stepbrother:stepsister, stepfather:stepmother, stepson:stepdaughter, uncle:aunt. — [questions-words.txt (word2vec mirror)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nicholas-leonard/word2vec/master/questions-words.txt) (counted locally: 23 × 22 = 506 lines)
- Accuracies, word2vec (CBOW, negative sampling), mean ± sd over three seeds: family 79.6 ± 1.5 on text8 (10 iterations) and 78.7 ± 2.2 on the 1-billion-word corpus (vs e.g. capital-common-countries 63.8/79.3, gram1-adjective-to-adverb 11.0/12.3; totals 42.1/57.2). — [Horn 2017, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02496)
- Another evaluation reports word2vec at about 68.18% on the family relation (described there as "gender inflections"). — [Fonarev/… arXiv 1412.6616 via search snippet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1412.6616) (not read directly; see Gaps)
- Bolukbasi et al. list daughter→son (93%/91% agreement with crowd gender words), mother→father (91%/85%) among the ten gender-defining pairs, i.e., kinship pairs are among the cleanest gender-direction estimators in word2vec Google News vectors. — [Bolukbasi et al. 2016, Figure 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)

**BATS (Gladkova, Drozd & Matsuoka 2016)**
- BATS has 40 categories, 99,200 questions, in four groups: inflectional morphology (I01–I10), derivational morphology (D01–D10), lexicographic semantics (L01 hypernyms–animals, L02 hypernyms–misc, L03 hyponyms–misc, L04 meronyms–substance, L05 meronyms–member, L06 meronyms–part-whole, L07 synonyms–intensity, L08 synonyms–exact, L09 antonyms–gradable, L10 antonyms–binary) and encyclopedic semantics (E01 capitals, E02 country:language, E03 UK city:county, E04 nationalities, E05 occupation, E06 animal young, E07 animal sounds, E08 animal shelter, E09 thing:color, E10 male:female (actor:actress)). There is no kinship/family category; "Categories E01 and E10 are based on the Google test". — [Gladkova et al. 2016, Table 1](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002.pdf)
- Difficulty: GloVe averaged 28.5% and the best SVD model 22.1% on BATS, vs 80.4% for GloVe on the Google set on the same 5B corpus; "neither of them achieves even 30% accuracy, suggesting that BATS is much more difficult than the Google test set." "The low accuracy on the lexicographic part of BATS is consistent with the findings of Köper et al. (2015)". — [Gladkova et al. 2016](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002.pdf)
- Per-category SVD accuracies (1000 dims, window 3): L01 hypernyms–animals 10%, L02 hypernyms–misc 4%, L03 hyponyms 8%, L04–L06 meronyms 0–5%, L10 binary antonyms 33% (the easiest lexicographic category), E10 male:female 25%. — [Gladkova et al. 2016, Table 2](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002.pdf)
- "A big factor is word frequency … Some categories could perform worse because they contain only low-frequency vocabulary". — [Gladkova et al. 2016](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002.pdf)

**Hinton 1986 — the family-tree network (features discovered by backprop)**
- Citation: Hinton, G. E. (1986). "Learning distributed representations of concepts." *Proc. 8th Annual Conf. of the Cognitive Science Society*, Amherst MA, pp. 1–12. — [bibliographic record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/hinton-learningdistributedrepresentationsofconcepts-1986); [PDF](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)
- Task: 12 relationships (father, mother, husband, wife, son, daughter, uncle, aunt, brother, sister, nephew, niece); "there are 104 instances of relationships in the two family trees" (an English tree and an isomorphic Italian tree, 12 people each); "We trained the network on 100 of these instances. The training involved 1500 sweeps through the 100 examples". — [Hinton 1986, §3](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)
- Architecture (Figure 5 caption): 24 input units for person 1 and 12 for the relationship, each group "totally connected to its own group of 6 units in the second layer. These two groups of 6 must learn to encode the input terms as distributed patterns of activity"; then a central layer of 12 units, a penultimate layer of 6, and 24 output units (one per person). — [Hinton 1986](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)
- Learned person features (Figure 6 and §3.1): "at least one unit (unit number 1) is primarily concerned with the distinction between English and Italian and that most of the other units ignore this distinction." "Unit 2 encodes which generation a person belongs to. Notice that the middle generation is encoded by an intermediate activity level. The network is never explicitly told that generation is a useful three-valued feature. It discovers this for itself by searching for features that make it easy to express the regularities of the domain." "Unit 6 encodes which branch of the family a person belongs to. Again, this is useful for expressing the regularities but is not at all explicit in the examples." — [Hinton 1986](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)
- On sex: "It is initially surprising that none of the 6 units encodes sex. This is probably because of the particular set of relationship terms that were used. Each of the 12 relationship terms completely determines the sex of person2 so the sex of person1 is redundant. Figure 7 shows that one of the 6 units which encodes the twelve possible relationships is entirely devoted to predicting the sex of person2." — [Hinton 1986](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)
- Generalisation: trained on 100 of 104, tested on 4; over two runs "the network got all four test cases correct and in the other case it got 3 out of 4". Input codes were deliberately local to remove "all local cues that might have suggested the appropriate features." — [Hinton 1986](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)
- Hinton's retrospective: the family-trees task showed "backpropagation can learn distributed representations", with learned features including nationality, generation and branch of the family tree. — [deeplearning.ai Hinton interview](https://learn.deeplearning.ai/specializations/deep-learning/lesson/yeck9/geoffrey-hinton-interview)
- Follow-up: Paccanaro & Hinton's Linear Relational Embedding used the same family-tree data and noted the 1986 multilayer net "has problems in generalizing when many triplets are missing from the training set". — [Paccanaro & Hinton, NIPS 2001](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2001/hash/814a9c18f5abff398787c9cfcbf3d80c-Abstract.html)

**Distributional study of kin terms (French), Tanguy, Fabre, Hathout & Ho-Dac 2024**
- Citation: "Le sens de la famille : analyse du vocabulaire de la parenté par les plongements de mots", JADT 2024. 25 nouns: beau-frère, beau-père, belle-fille, belle-mère, belle-soeur, cousin, cousine, épouse, époux, fille, fils, frère, gendre, grand-mère, grand-père, mari, mère, neveu, nièce, oncle, père, petit-fils, petite-fille, soeur, tante (frequency ≥ 100 in every corpus; *femme* excluded because its dominant sense is 'female person'). — [Tanguy et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)
- Corpora/models: five corpora (FrWaC web, Frantext literary, Le Monde press, Revues.org scientific, Wikipédia) lemmatised and neutralised for grammatical gender; SGNS/word2vec models trained per corpus and concatenated (1500 dims per corpus; a global 7500-dim model). — [Tanguy et al. 2024, §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)
- Cohesion: among the 10 nearest neighbours of the 25 terms, 81% are themselves in the list (92.8% in the global model; from 95% for *oncle* down to 41.7% for *fille*); including informal/generic family words, 93%. — [Tanguy et al. 2024, §3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)
- Structure (t-SNE): systematic adjacency of pairs differing only in gender (frère–soeur), plus groupings for alliance (belle-mère, gendre…), ascendance (mère, grand-père, tante…) and descendance (fils, petite-fille…); in Wikipédia gender creates a clean partition; in Frantext the ascendant/descendant split is prominent; *fille* is isolated by polysemy and pulled away from *fils*; époux/épouse are inseparable in every corpus; "relatively indirect relations such as cousin, cousine, nièce and neveu tend to sit in median zones or at the intersection of several groupings." — [Tanguy et al. 2024, §3.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)
- Feature regression: terms coded on five componential features (genre; ascendance = number of ascending links; descendance; germanité = sibling link; alliance), ambiguous terms averaged (oncle alliance = 0.5). Regressing cosine similarity of the 300 pairs on absolute feature differences: every feature negatively and significantly related; adjusted R² = 0.238 (Frantext), 0.109 (FrWaC), 0.375 (Le Monde), 0.297 (Revues.org), 0.363 (Wikipédia), 0.350 (global). Relative R² loss on ablation: gender is the most important feature overall (−0.50 in Wikipédia, −0.32 global), alliance dominates in FrWaC (−0.40) and Le Monde (−0.41), ascendance in Frantext (−0.35); descendance and siblinghood are the weakest. — [Tanguy et al. 2024, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)

### Inferences
- Benchmarks only ever check the sex axis within kinship; the generation and lineality axes of the feature table have essentially no published offset-analogy numbers, so any report measurement of father→grandfather or father→uncle offsets would be new rather than a replication.
- BATS's very low lexicographic scores (hypernymy 4–10%) suggest that hierarchical/ordered relations are hard for vector offset even when the embedding neighbourhood is semantically coherent; generation is an ordered relation of the same kind, so one should expect it to show up as a *direction in a local PCA* more readily than as a clean constant offset.
- Hinton's result is the cleanest precedent for the claim that a learner discovers generation and branch as separate axes; it also predicts that an axis will be absent when it is redundant given the other inputs (sex was not learned because the relation term fixed it) — a caveat for reading absent axes in embeddings.
- Tanguy et al.'s R² values (max ~0.37) indicate that even in a tight, cohesive cluster, well under half of the distributional similarity variance is explained by the classical features; corpus genre changes which feature dominates.

### Gaps
- No paper found that probes generation (father→grandfather) or lineal/collateral (father→uncle) as vector offsets; searches on "kinship"/"kin terms" + "word embeddings"/"analogy" returned only the Tanguy et al. study. Treat as an open gap rather than evidence of absence.
- The 68.18% family figure for word2vec is from a search snippet of arXiv 1412.6616, not read directly.
- Mikolov et al.'s own per-category family accuracy was not verified from the primary paper; the Horn 2017 table is the primary number cited here.
- GloVe's BATS E10 (male:female) accuracy is shown only in a figure in the BATS paper; the number is not recoverable from text (SVD's is 25%).
- The exact gensim/word2vec hyperparameters and the way Tanguy et al. concatenated models were only partly recoverable from the French text; "1500 dimensions per corpus / 7500 global" is as stated but the construction details may be simplified here.

## Key Question 4: Psychological scaling (triads / MDS / correspondence analysis) of kinship similarity — precedent for reading eigen-axes

### Takeaway
The kinship domain is the textbook case of recovering a semantic field's axes from human similarity judgments: Romney & D'Andrade's 1964 triads over 8 male terms supported a three-dimensional model (generation distance, lineality, reciprocity), and Romney's later judged-similarity/correspondence-analysis work on English kin terms found that a common multidimensional space accounts for 70–90% of the variability and that 122 individuals hold "virtually identical cognitive representations". The precise axes and dimension count of the 1990s correspondence analyses could not be verified from a primary text. Nerlove & Romney 1967 is not an MDS study of English terms but a cross-cultural typology of sibling terminology.

### Cited Findings
- Romney & D'Andrade's three-dimensional model (generation 3 values × lineality 2 × reciprocity 2) vs Wallace–Atkins's two-dimensional (generation 5 × lineality 3) model for male terms; triads fit .86/.84 vs .33/.40 (see KQ1). — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- The NAS memoir credits Romney & D'Andrade with introducing triadic similarity judgments "as a way to estimate the cognitive structure for terms in a domain", and states the R–D model was three-dimensional while W–A was two-dimensional, with the similarity data fitting R–D better. — [NAS memoir, A. Kimball Romney](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Romney-A.-Kimball.pdf)
- Romney & Wexler re-analysed the triads data at the individual level using MDS of respondent-by-respondent agreement (35 males, 35 females), finding a core group closely matching R–D and no individuals resembling W–A. — [analytictech](http://www.analytictech.com/mb870/componential.htm)
- Romney, Boyd, Moore, Batchelder & Brazill (1996), "Culture as shared cognitive representations", *PNAS* 93: 4699–4705: judged similarity among English kinship terms, "six independent measurements from each of 122 individuals", placed by correspondence analysis in a common multidimensional space; the model "accounts for 70–90% of the total variability"; conclusion that individuals share "virtually identical cognitive representations" of the semantic structure, expected to generalise to domains like animals and emotions. — [PMC39342 abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC39342)
- A later fMRI paper summarises Romney et al. as having "selected 15 kinship terms in English and conducted a semantic structure analysis" whose structure "was consistent with genetic relatedness"; it also notes English has 15 kin terms vs 33 in Chinese. — [Wang et al., PMC4685275](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4685275/)
- Widmer & Romney (1999) extended the triads/Euclidean-representation method to step-terms; a secondary summary reports the result "matches predictions from the componential analysis paradigm" and that the consanguineal-vs-step distinction is crucial. — [Widmer, Romney & Boyd 1999 (Wiley record)](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1525/aa.1999.101.2.374) (abstract only via search; page returned 403)
- Nerlove & Romney (1967), "Sibling terminology and cross-sex behavior", *American Anthropologist* 69: 179–187, builds a cross-cultural typology of sibling terminologies and tests whether a long post-partum sex taboo predicts a primary cross-parallel component in sibling terms (supported, p<.001); it is not an MDS of English kin terms. — [HRAF record](https://hraf.yale.edu/documents/82)
- Cross-linguistic efficiency work also cites Nerlove & Romney 1967 alongside Murdock 1949 and Kemp & Regier 2012 as sources on how kin terms vary in the semantic distinctions they encode. — [Hallam et al. 2025](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1fg7b7hn)

### Inferences
- The R–D triads result is the best-documented "answer key" for human judgments: the dominant human axes for male terms are generation *distance* (0/1/2), lineality (direct/collateral) and reciprocity (ascending/descending), with sex layered on top for the full set. If a local PCA of embeddings yields a sex axis first, that would already differ from the human triads structure (where sex was held constant), so the comparison should be made within-sex as well as across.
- The 70–90% variance figure from correspondence analysis of judged similarity is a useful benchmark number: a low-dimensional (likely 2–3 axis) space suffices for human kinship judgments.

### Gaps
- The full text of Romney et al. 1996 (PNAS) and Romney et al. 2000 (PNAS, English vs Chinese) could not be retrieved (NCBI and Europe PMC both served interstitial/Cloudflare pages to the fetcher). The number of dimensions retained and their interpretation (generation / sex / lineality) are therefore unverified; only the abstract-level facts above are cited.
- The task brief's statement that MDS "typically yields axes of generation and sex/lineality" is consistent with the componential models but I found no primary source stating the axis labels of an MDS/correspondence solution explicitly.
- Romney & Wexler's individual-differences re-analysis is known only from the analytictech course page (the original is in Romney, Shepard & Nerlove's *Multidimensional Scaling* volumes, 1972, which I could not access).

## Key Question 5: What embeddings should be expected to do differently from the feature table

### Takeaway
Three documented distortions should be anticipated: (1) gender is the single dominant, near-linear direction in word2vec/GloVe space and kinship pairs are among its purest carriers (Bolukbasi et al.), and it was also the strongest explanatory feature in the French distributional study, so sex will likely be the first local axis even though human triads structure is organised by generation/lineality; (2) polysemy pulls specific terms out of the cluster (Tanguy et al.'s *fille* 'daughter/girl'; Bolukbasi's note that *grandfather* has a verb sense and that *grandmother/grandfather* carry a "wisdom" association); (3) frequency imbalance (parents are referred to far more than children or niblings; low-frequency BATS categories score worse) will degrade the rarer collateral terms (nephew, niece, cousin), which Tanguy et al. found sitting in "median zones" between clusters.

### Cited Findings
- Gender as a dominant linear direction: Bolukbasi et al. combine ten gender pair difference vectors (she–he, her–his, woman–man, Mary–John, herself–himself, daughter–son, mother–father, gal–guy, girl–boy, female–male) and "computed its principal components (PCs). As Figure 6 shows, there is a single direction that explains the majority of variance in these vectors. The first eigenvalue is significantly larger than the rest." — [Bolukbasi et al. 2016, §5.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)
- Kinship pairs are among the best gender classifiers: daughter→son classifies crowd-sourced definitional/stereotypical gender words at 93%/91%, mother→father at 91%/85%, versus she→he 92%/89%. — [Bolukbasi et al. 2016, Figure 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)
- Stereotyped kinship analogies: the Google News word2vec embedding "outputs that a father is to a doctor as a mother is to a nurse." — [Bolukbasi et al. 2016, §1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)
- Extra non-gender structure and polysemy within kinship: "grandmother and grandfather are both closer to wisdom than gal and guy are, which does not reflect a gender difference"; and the authors note the verb sense "to grandfather a regulation" as a reason grandfather and grandmother should not be made perfectly symmetric. — [Bolukbasi et al. 2016, §§7–8](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)
- Later work confirms gender bias "is in fact well captured by a linear subspace" even when a non-linear (kernelised) alternative is tested. — [Vargas & Cotterell 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.09435.pdf)
- Gender is the strongest feature explaining distributional similarity among French kin terms overall (relative R² loss −0.32 in the global model, −0.50 in Wikipédia), while the corpus genre shifts which feature dominates (alliance in press/web, ascendance in literature). — [Tanguy et al. 2024, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)
- Polysemy effects: *fille* ('daughter'/'girl') is the least cohesive term (41.7% of its nearest neighbours are kin terms vs 95% for *oncle*) and "tends to be isolated from the group … and pulled away from *fils*"; *femme* ('wife'/'woman') had to be excluded entirely. — [Tanguy et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)
- Peripheral collateral terms: "relatively indirect relations such as cousin, cousine, nièce and neveu tend to sit in median zones or at the intersection of several groupings." — [Tanguy et al. 2024, §3.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19729)
- Frequency: Kemp & Regier's need probabilities (from COCA and the German Reference Corpus) show "references to parents should tend to be more common across the whole society" than references to children, because everyone has parents but not everyone has children. — [Kemp & Regier 2012](https://terry-regier.github.io/lclab/papers/kinship-science-2012.PDF). BATS: "A big factor is word frequency … Some categories could perform worse because they contain only low-frequency vocabulary". — [Gladkova et al. 2016](https://aclanthology.org/N16-2002.pdf)
- Redundant axes can fail to appear: in Hinton's network no person unit encoded sex because the relation terms already determined it. — [Hinton 1986](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/families.pdf)
- The Google "family" category mixes true kin terms with non-kin gender pairs (he:she, king:queen, policeman:policewoman, groom:bride), so its ~79% accuracy measures gender offset, not kinship structure. — [questions-words.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nicholas-leonard/word2vec/master/questions-words.txt)

### Inferences
- Expect a local PCA of the ~15 English terms to put sex first (large, clean, linear), with generation emerging as a second, ordered axis and lineality as a weaker third; this is the reverse of the human triads salience ordering, and the report should present that as a predicted divergence rather than a failure.
- English-specific polysemy to anticipate (not sourced; flagged): *father* (priest, "Founding Fathers"), *son* ("Son of God", address term), *brother/sister* (religious orders, solidarity, nursing), *uncle* ("Uncle Sam", "say uncle"), *cousin* (loose relatedness, "country cousin"), *mother* (metaphoric "mother of…"). These senses would be expected to pull father/son/brother/sister toward religious or institutional neighbourhoods, analogous to the French *fille* effect.
- Because every human analysis holds affinal/step terms apart (consanguineal core only), including husband/wife, in-laws or step-terms in the embedding cluster will add an alliance axis that the 15-term answer key does not contain — Tanguy et al. found alliance to be the dominant feature in two of five corpora.

### Gaps
- No source was found giving corpus frequencies for the 15 English terms side by side; the frequency-asymmetry argument rests on Kemp & Regier's need-probability reasoning and BATS's general remark. A report could compute them directly from a frequency list.
- The English polysemy list above is my inference; the only sourced polysemy cases are French *fille*/*femme* and English *grandfather* (verb) / grandparent–wisdom association.
- No study was found that measures how much of the first principal component of a kinship cluster in English embeddings is gender; Bolukbasi's PCA is over gender *difference vectors*, not over kinship word vectors themselves.
